## Meat

### Beef

OLD line 1: 100% grass-fed or grass-finished beef, no added hormones, a certifier’s seal.
OLD line 2: ‘100% grass-fed’ and ‘grass-finished’ mean the same thing. Either one is the claim.
NEW line 1: You’re looking for grass-finished beef, raised on grass all the way.
NEW line 2: On the label, “100% grass-fed” or “grass-finished” tells you the finish. Look for no added hormones and a certifier’s seal, too.

### Rotisserie chicken from the hot case

OLD line 1: Rotisserie chicken with salt and spices, without phosphate or starch.
OLD line 2: Read the sticker, not the name. ‘Seasoned’, ‘original’ and ‘traditional’ are marketing; the ingredient list is regulated.
NEW line 1: The plain rotisserie chicken is the one to bring home.
NEW line 2: Turn to the sticker: chicken, salt and spices make a plain bird. “Seasoned”, “original” and “traditional” alone tell you little.

### Steak

OLD line 1: Whole chuck or round, without needles or blades pushed through.
OLD line 2: Chuck is shoulder and round is rear leg: the working muscles.
NEW line 1: Whole chuck or round gives you beef without needles or blades.
NEW line 2: The name tells you whether needles or blades went through it: look for “mechanically”, “needle” or “blade” tenderized.

### Ground beef

OLD line 1: Organ blend with each organ’s percentage first, then nothing-added ground beef.
OLD line 2: Read the name: ‘ground beef’ may not have beef fat added as such; ‘hamburger’ may.
NEW line 1: A beef organ blend works when you know each organ’s share.
NEW line 2: Check the printed percentages for liver and heart; they tell you how much organ is folded into the grind.

### Eggs

OLD line 1: Eggs with pasture-raised, organic, corn- and soy-free on one carton.
OLD line 2: ‘Soy-free’ and ‘corn-free’ are the only words on the carton about the ration.
NEW line 1: For eggs, organic feed and pasture-raised belong on the same carton.
NEW line 2: On one carton, you want pasture-raised, organic, corn-free and soy-free. The feed words tell you what went into the hens’ trough.

### Judging meat at the case

OLD line 1: The meat pack with the newest ‘packaged on’ date.
OLD line 2: Not slimy, and no off smell. Both, not either.
NEW line 1: Your meat pick starts with the newest “packaged on” date.
NEW line 2: Feel the pack and notice any smell: slime or an off smell means leave it. Purple vacuum-packed beef alone isn’t spoilage.

### What to ask the butcher

OLD line 1: Beef soup bones and a whole chuck, freshly ground.
OLD line 2: Ask what came in today.
NEW line 1: The butcher can grind a whole beef chuck just for you.
NEW line 2: Ask what arrived today, and about soup bones while you’re there. The counter can tell you when the meat was cut.

### Chicken

OLD line 1: Air-chilled whole chicken first, then bone-in, skin-on thighs.
OLD line 2: Whole bird: lowest cost per pound, and the carcass makes stock.
NEW line 1: Air-chilled whole chicken gives you thighs, breast and bones for stock.
NEW line 2: You’ll find “air-chilled” printed on the pack. If you’re choosing thighs, bone-in and skin-on give you more room before they dry out.

### Pork cuts and ‘solution added’ pork

OLD line 1: Plain pork, with no added-solution percentage on its label.
OLD line 2: Read for ‘contains X% added solution’. That is injected water and salt.
NEW line 1: Plain pork is the one without an injected solution.
NEW line 2: Beside the name, “contains X% added solution” means water and salt were injected; that’s the line to check here.

### Whole turkey

OLD line 1: Plain whole turkey, without an injected basting solution.
OLD line 2: Read the solution ingredients beside the product name.
NEW line 1: A plain whole turkey gives you a bird with nothing added.
NEW line 2: Next to the name, check for a basting solution and its ingredients. Broth, stock, fat or water can be added that way.

### Ground turkey

OLD line 1: Plain ground turkey, with ingredients and fat content on the label.
OLD line 2: Choose a formulation listing turkey without added seasonings or a flavoring solution.
NEW line 1: Plain ground turkey lets you choose the fat content yourself.
NEW line 2: Compare the packs’ fat lines; the grinds can differ. Then check for turkey without added seasonings or a flavoring solution.

### Chicken breast

OLD line 1: Plain bone-in, skin-on breast before boneless skinless; no added solution.
OLD line 2: Read the full product name for the solution percentage and ingredients.
NEW line 1: Chicken breast cooks better with its bone and skin still on.
NEW line 2: Read beside the product name for an added solution’s percentage and ingredients; a plain breast has none, fresh or frozen.

NEW line 1: Duck and quail eggs should come from the refrigerated case.
NEW line 2: Open this carton for a look at the shells: clean and uncracked. Plan to cook both white and yolk solid.

### Lamb and goat

### Organ meats

OLD line 1: Organ meats for cooking within one to two days.

## Bread

### Sandwich bread

OLD line 1: Whole wheat sandwich bread, with the grain’s bran and germ intact.
OLD line 2: Check for whole wheat flour in the ingredients.
NEW line 1: Whole wheat sandwich bread keeps the whole grain in your loaf.
NEW line 2: The flour name is your guide: look for “whole wheat” in the ingredients, which keeps the bran and germ together.

### Sourdough bread

OLD line 1: Sourdough raised with a starter of wild yeast and bacteria.
OLD line 2: Look for flour, water, salt, and a culture or starter.
NEW line 1: Sourdough should get its rise from a starter.
NEW line 2: Ask the baker how the dough rises. Flour, water, salt and a culture or starter tell you more than sour flavor alone.

### Bagels

OLD line 1: Bagels boiled before the oven, with their ingredients named.
OLD line 2: Read the ingredient list on the bag, or ask the bakery for it.
NEW line 1: Your bagel should have a boil before it reaches the oven.
NEW line 2: At the bakery, ask about boiling and the ingredient list. The bath sets the outside; the shape alone won’t tell you.

### Corn vs. flour tortillas

OLD line 1: Corn tortillas made with lime-treated corn, water and salt.
OLD line 2: Read the list for masa or masa harina, water, and salt.
NEW line 1: Corn tortillas made from masa are the ones to reach for.
NEW line 2: In the ingredients, masa or masa harina means lime-treated corn; water and salt complete it. Check for wheat flour added alongside.

### Sprouted grain bread

OLD line 1: Sprouted whole grains first in your bread, without added flour.
OLD line 2: Read the first ingredients for sprouted whole grains.
NEW line 1: Sprouted whole grain bread gives you a denser, heavier loaf.
NEW line 2: In the cold or freezer case, check for sprouted whole grains first, without added flour. Some loaves blend the two.

### Gluten-free bread

OLD line 1: Gluten-free labeled bread, with its flours and binder checked.
OLD line 2: Check for one of the four gluten-free claim wordings.
NEW line 1: For gluten-free bread, start with the claim on the package.
NEW line 2: Then read the flours, starches and gum binder. Those replace wheat and give the loaf some of the stretch gluten provides.

### The bread aisle

OLD line 2: Read the first ingredient for “whole”.
NEW line 1: Your usual bread type is the starting point for whole grain.
NEW line 2: Now turn to the first flour and look for “whole”. The Whole Grain Stamp helps distinguish whole from refined grain.

## Produce

### Produce

OLD line 1: Organic produce eaten whole; conventional fruit and vegetables you peel.
OLD line 2: Ask one question at the shelf: does the skin get eaten? Berries, greens, apples, grapes and potatoes, yes. That is where the money goes.
NEW line 1: Organic makes most sense for produce you’ll eat whole.
NEW line 2: Think about the peel here: berries and apples keep theirs. Conventional fruit you peel leaves surface residue behind with the skin.

### Frozen vs. fresh produce

OLD line 1: Frozen vegetables, picked ripe and frozen within hours.
OLD line 2: Check the bag lists just the vegetable/fruit. No sauces or added sugar.
NEW line 1: Plain frozen vegetables give you produce held at its picked ripeness.
NEW line 2: Inside this bag, you want just fruit or vegetables, without sauce or added sugar; freezing needs no additives.

### Freezing what is about to turn

OLD line 1: Briefly scalded vegetables for the freezer; fruit goes in raw.
OLD line 2: Sixty seconds in boiling water, then straight into ice water to stop the cooking.
NEW line 1: Vegetables for freezing need a brief scald; fruit can go raw.
NEW line 2: For those beans, plan sixty seconds in boiling water, then ice water. The scald keeps them from turning gray and tasting of hay.

### Buying produce in season

OLD line 1: In-season produce, picked closer to ripe with less distance to travel.
OLD line 2: The cheapest, most abundant produce this week is usually what’s in season.
NEW line 1: Produce in season reaches you closer to ripe.
NEW line 2: Those abundant piles give you a clue to what’s in season locally. It usually has less distance to travel.

### Washing produce

OLD line 1: Plain cold water for produce, as effective as a produce wash.
OLD line 2: Rinse under cold running water; rub firm produce or use a brush.
NEW line 1: Plain cold water is all your produce needs for washing.
NEW line 2: Before adding a produce wash, remember that running water and rubbing lift dirt; bottled washes haven’t proved better.

### Getting residue off conventional produce

OLD line 1: Baking soda water for residue that a plain rinse leaves.
OLD line 2: A teaspoon of baking soda per two cups of water, enough to cover the fruit.
NEW line 1: Baking soda gives thin-skinned fruit a soak beyond a rinse.
NEW line 2: With conventional apples, grapes or berries, plan a teaspoon in two cups of water. It works on some surface residue.

### Pre-cut and pre-washed produce

OLD line 1: Whole produce before pre-cut: cutting starts vitamin loss and browning.
OLD line 2: Whole produce is cheaper per pound and keeps far longer.
NEW line 1: Whole produce gives you more time to use it.
NEW line 2: At the cut-fruit case, think about the next couple of days. An opened pack spoils quickly; whole fruit can wait.

### How to pick it, item by item

OLD line 1: The heavier of two same-size pieces of fruit for more juice.
OLD line 2: Cantaloupe: creamy gold under the netting, a sweet smell at the stem end, slight give.
NEW line 1: The fruit that feels heavier for its size usually has more juice.
NEW line 2: Hold two of the same size, one in each hand. The extra weight tells you more about water than color does.

### Berries

OLD line 1: Organic berries in a cold carton with a dry, clean underside.
OLD line 2: A dry, unstained bottom on the carton. That is the whole check.
NEW line 1: Organic berries should be ready to eat today.
NEW line 2: Turn the cold carton over: you want a dry, unstained bottom and no fuzzy berries. The top can hide older fruit.

### Making produce last the week

OLD line 1: Unwashed produce for storing; wash it when you use it.
OLD line 2: Separate the gas producers (apples, bananas, avocados, tomatoes, stone fruit) from greens and berries.
NEW line 1: Unwashed produce will keep better until you’re ready to use it.
NEW line 2: As you bag greens and berries, keep apples and bananas apart from them; their ripening gas speeds things along.

### Reviving wilted greens

OLD line 1: Limp greens without decay, ready for a cold-water soak.
OLD line 2: Thirty minutes in cold water covers most of it. An hour for the worst of them.
NEW line 1: Limp greens can perk up, as long as there’s no decay.
NEW line 2: Check whether the leaves are just limp or breaking down. Cold water firms limp ones in thirty minutes, an hour for the worst.

### Sprouts

OLD line 1: Cold-case sprouts; warm, damp growing conditions also suit bacteria.
OLD line 2: The refrigerated case only. Room-temperature sprouts have been left in growing conditions.
NEW line 1: Your sprouts belong in a cold clamshell with crisp stems.
NEW line 2: Here in the refrigerated case, check for white roots and no sour smell. Warm, damp growing conditions also suit bacteria.

### Root vegetables: carrots, potatoes, beets

OLD line 1: Firm organic potatoes, conventional carrots, with nothing soft or sprouting.
OLD line 2: Potatoes: firm, with no bruises, nicks, or soft spots.
NEW line 1: Organic potatoes and conventional carrots should feel solid in your hand.
NEW line 2: Give each root a gentle press: no soft spots, nicks or sprouts. Wilted beet leaves matter less than a firm root.

### Broccoli, cauliflower, and cabbage

OLD line 1: Conventional cabbage and cauliflower with tight heads and no soft spots.
OLD line 2: Broccoli: dark green florets, closed tight, on stems that are not thick and tough.
NEW line 1: Tight heads are your cue for conventional cabbage and cauliflower.
NEW line 2: For broccoli alongside them, look for closed, dark green florets; open or yellowish ones have gone past fresh.

### Onions and garlic

OLD line 1: Conventional onions and garlic: firm, dry, papery skins, no sprouts or mold.
OLD line 2: Onions firm for their size, with little to no scent.
NEW line 1: Conventional onions and garlic should feel firm in your hand.
NEW line 2: At the neck, you want dry, papery skin and little scent. Softness, sprouts or mold are reasons to leave a bulb.

### Leafy greens: lettuce, spinach, kale

OLD line 1: Crisp organic greens, evenly colored, without wilt or yellowing.
OLD line 2: Lettuce: crisp leaves, bright to dark green outside and pale at the heart, free of decay or damage.
NEW line 1: Organic greens with leaves that stand crisp are the ones.
NEW line 2: A glance through the bunch helps: even green, no yellowing or decay. Smaller kale leaves will be milder and more tender.

### Bell peppers

OLD line 1: Firm organic peppers, evenly colored, without cracks or soft spots.
OLD line 2: Firm, with uniform shape, size, and color for the variety.
NEW line 1: Organic peppers should feel firm beneath your fingers.
NEW line 2: Turn each pepper to check for even color and no cracks or soft spots. Wrinkling tells you it has lost water.

### Apples and pears

OLD line 1: Unbruised organic apples and pears, with no spoiled fruit alongside.
OLD line 2: Apples: firm and crisp, free of bruising, decay, cracks, and insect injury.
NEW line 1: Sound organic apples and pears are the fruit to take home.
NEW line 2: Check each side for bruises and decay. Apples should feel crisp; a firm green Bartlett pear can still ripen at home.

### Citrus: oranges, lemons, limes

OLD line 2: Oranges: even color, firm, smooth, no decay, bruising, or blemishes.
NEW line 1: Conventional citrus works here; organic is the pick when you’ll use zest.
NEW line 2: Feel the rind for firmness and smoothness, without shriveling. A partly green orange can be mature; yellow limes carry more juice.

NEW line 2: Under a peach’s blush, green should have turned yellow. Check plums and cherries for full color too, and leave bruised fruit behind.
