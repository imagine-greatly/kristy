# Card style samples (2026-10-17, for Devon's read)

The style: the headline names the best option in the label words you will find. The why says
what makes it better. Kristy's take is the shelf knowledge most shoppers miss. Steps go best,
close second, skip. Every fact comes from the entry's own material; no new claims.
Not applied to the KB.

## Beef
**Headline:** 100% grass-fed or grass-finished beef, no added hormones, with a certifier's seal.
**Why:** Both labels mean the animal ate grass after weaning, never grain, and never stood in a feedlot. That grass finish gives you leaner beef with a different fat balance.
**Kristy's take:** The words on the label are the claim; pasture artwork is not. USDA withdrew its grass-fed standard in 2016, so a certifier's seal is what makes "grass-fed" mean something. When grass-finished is not on the shelf, organic grain-finished is your floor.
**Steps:**
1. "100% grass-fed" or "grass-finished", backed by a certifier's seal.
2. None on the shelf: organic grain-finished.
3. At the counter, ask what it was finished on, and whose definition.

## Ground beef
**Headline:** A beef organ blend with each organ's percentage printed, or 100% grass-fed ground beef with nothing added.
**Why:** A blend folds liver and heart into a grind you already cook with, and the printed shares tell you how much of each. Plain "ground beef" cannot have added water, phosphates, binders or extra fat, so the list should read beef, with seasoning at most.
**Kristy's take:** Read the name before anything else. "Hamburger" is allowed added beef fat; "ground beef" is not. An "organ blend" or "ancestral" pack with no percentages anywhere on it is a pass, not a bargain.
**Steps:**
1. Organ blend, 100% grass-fed: each organ named with its own percentage.
2. Next: 100% grass-fed ground beef, ingredients just beef.
3. Skip "hamburger", which allows added beef fat.

## Steak
**Headline:** A 100% grass-fed or grass-finished whole cut, not mechanically tenderized.
**Why:** Start with how the animal was raised; the cut comes second. A whole cut that no needles or blades went through is still the muscle as it was. Chuck and round are the lowest-cost way into grass-finished beef.
**Kristy's take:** Prime, Choice and Select grade marbling, not how the animal lived, and "Angus" is not a grade at all. Read the sourcing words first, then the name: "mechanically", "needle" or "blade tenderized" means the cut was pushed through.
**Steps:**
1. "100% grass-fed" or "grass-finished", backed by a certifier's seal.
2. Pass on any name with "mechanically", "needle" or "blade" tenderized.
3. Chuck (shoulder) or round (rear leg) for grass-finished at the lowest cost.

## Eggs
**Headline:** Pasture-raised, organic, corn- and soy-free eggs, all on one carton.
**Why:** A hen eats what she is given. Pasture tells you about her life; organic and corn- and soy-free tell you about her feed. You want both.
**Kristy's take:** Most supermarket cartons never mention feed, the big pasture-raised brands included, so organic is the rung you can reliably buy there. Soy-free lives at farm stands and farmers markets; ask the person who keeps the hens. Then find the three-digit pack date: closer to today is fresher.
**Steps:**
1. Pasture-raised, organic, corn- and soy-free, with Certified Humane pasture-raised or Animal Welfare Approved.
2. In the supermarket: pasture-raised organic.
3. Skip "cage-free" and "vegetarian-fed"; neither promises time outdoors.

## Milk
**Headline:** Raw grass-fed whole milk from a farm you can name, or low-temp pasteurized 100% grass-fed whole milk.
**Why:** Every step between the cow and the carton changes milk: heat, homogenizing, skimming. Whole keeps its milkfat, and 100% grass-fed means the cows ate grass and hay, never grain.
**Kristy's take:** The date tells you the heat. A date months out means ultra-pasteurized, about 280°F; skip it. Cream-top means non-homogenized, so shake it. It is still pasteurized, only the fat was left alone.
**Steps:**
1. Raw, from a farm you can name.
2. Next: "vat pasteurized" or "low-temperature" with 100% grass-fed, cream-top if you find it.
3. Skip "ultra-pasteurized" or "UHT".

## Cheese
**Headline:** Real cheese in a block, with a list that reads milk, cultures, salt and enzymes or rennet.
**Why:** That is all cheese needs. Bagged shreds add potato starch, powdered cellulose and natamycin to keep them apart, which is also why they melt poorly.
**Kristy's take:** Read the front name first. "Cheese product", "cheese food" or "pasteurized process" means emulsifiers blended in, and a "cheese product" needs only 51% cheese. Buy the block and grate it; it costs less per pound, too.
**Steps:**
1. The front name says cheese: no "product", "food" or "process".
2. A short list: milk, salt, cultures, enzymes or rennet.
3. The block over the bag.
