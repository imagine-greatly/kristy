Nearly all potato samples contain pesticide not approved for use in the EU
==========================================================================

UPDATED TUESDAY, MARCH 24, 2026

Potatoes – the [most consumed](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/chart-detail?chartId=95421#:~:text=If%20mashed%20potatoes%20are%20on,pounds%20per%20person%20in%202017.) vegetable in the U.S. – remain on this year’s Dirty Dozen™, rising one spot to 11th place. The reason: high concentrations of chlorpropham, a plant growth regulator banned in the European Union due to health concerns.

From 2022 to 2024, the Agriculture Department collected and tested more than 1,300 potato samples. They washed and scrubbed the samples then tested for pesticide residues. Ninety percent of the samples contained chlorpropham residues.

The average concentration across all samples was nearly three parts per million – high concentrations relative to most other pesticides detected in produce. And the concentrations are twice the amount the Environmental Protection Agency used when it last assessed [chlorpropham](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://www.regulations.gov/document/EPA-HQ-OPP-2010-0923-0026), in 2017.

The chemical was detected in concentrations over 5 parts per million, or ppm, in just 21% of potato samples. Since 1990, chlorpropham has mostly been used on potatoes in the U.S. to prevent them from sprouting during storage.

[In 2019,](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?qid=1560932540431&uri=CELEX:32019R0989) the European Union rolled back its approval of chlorpropham. That decision followed a [2017 risk assessment](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/epdf/10.2903/j.efsa.2017.4903), by the European Food Safety Authority, finding that people, especially children, who eat potatoes and other food sprayed with chlorpropham may be exposed to the chemical – and its breakdown product 3-chloroaniline – at levels above those regulators consider won’t cause any health harms.

The assessment also emphasized more data were needed to assess chlorpropham's potential hormone-disrupting effects.

Big questions about chlorpropham
--------------------------------

Both U.S. and European regulators identified changes in blood cells and harm to the thyroid as the primary toxicological effects associated with exposure to chlorpropham in animal studies submitted to agencies in the U.S. and EU by pesticide companies.

Peer-reviewed studies also suggest [chlorpropham may have hormone-disrupting properties](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0269749123004396), though more research is needed.

The 2017 [assessment](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.2903/j.efsa.2017.4903) by the European Food Safety Authority, or EFSA, as well as [peer-reviewed studies](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://www.mdpi.com/2297-8739/9/3/77) have noted that potato storage and processing, like boiling, roasting or frying, can decrease chlorpropham levels in potatoes.

EFSA also raised concerns about the degradation of chlorpropham into 3-chloroaniline, a breakdown product structurally similar to the cancer-causing compound 4-chloroaniline.

And the EPA’s assessment indicated 3-chloroaniline can form in fried potatoes. EFSA found that health risks associated with 3-chloroanaline exposure exceeded the risks associated with chlorpropham and requested more data on 3-chloroaniline formation.

The EPA also found that [children were the most exposed to chlorpropham of any age group,](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://www.regulations.gov/document/EPA-HQ-OPP-2010-0923-0027) though these levels did not exceed the agency’s level of concern. Toxic chemical exposures can be especially harmful to children, whose bodies are still developing and who consume more food per measurement of body weight than adults.

Uncertainty about harms to people
---------------------------------

Despite the prevalence of chlorpropham on potatoes, and the high consumption rates in the U.S., there are no peer-reviewed academic studies assess the effects of chlorpropham exposure in people.

One study identified [chlorpropham as the most frequently detected pesticide](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1438463922001882?via%3Dihub) in human urine samples, with higher concentrations observed in children than in adults. The study, conducted before chlorpropham was phased out in the EU, focused on 2,000 participants from five EU countries.

Organic potatoes may not be grown with chlorpropham. But USDA tests of 28 organic samples of potatoes found residues on 12, likely due to cross-contamination during storage with conventional potatoes. The average concentration on organic samples was 0.196 ppm.

[Exposure to chlorpropham was lower](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0013935123020200?via%3Dihub) in individuals from the Netherlands and Switzerland who reported eating large quantities of organic fruits and vegetables.

What about sweet potatoes?
--------------------------

Unlike white potatoes, conventional sweet potatoes are not sprayed with chlorpropham. Less than 1% of sweet potato samples had any detectable chlorpropham, and these were always extremely low levels. And sweet potatoes rank only three spots off the Clean Fifteen in EWG’s full list of fruit and vegetable rankings – they are a less-contaminated alternative to potatoes.

Just under 60% of sweet potato samples tested by the USDA contained any pesticides residues, compared to the 95% of potato samples. The overall pesticide toxicity was also lower for sweet potatoes than for potatoes.

Consumption of sweet potatoes has been increasing in the U.S., according to USDA [data.](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/chart-detail?chartId=99880)

Reducing your exposure
----------------------

Consumption of fresh fruits and vegetables is important for a healthy diet. They are rich in vitamins, minerals and antioxidants, which help strengthen the immune system, protect heart health and lower the risk of chronic disease.

If you’d like to reduce pesticide exposure, rather than give up potatoes, consider these steps:.

EWG recommends continuing to eat potatoes, as well as other fruits and vegetables. Consider choosing sweet potatoes or if white potatoes are a favorite food, consider choosing organic when possible, to minimize exposure to chlorpropham. While organic potatoes do test positive for chlorpropham because of cross-contamination, the levels were much lower in concentration than in conventional potatoes.

Make sure to wash all produce with water before consuming to help reduce pesticide levels, as well as bacteria. Peeling potatoes can also reduce chlorpropham levels but may also reduce the nutritional value. For more information on reducing pesticide exposure through washing, refer to [EWG’s Fruit and Vegetable Washing Guide](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://www.ewg.org/consumer-guides/ewgs-guide-washing-produce).

About EWG’s Shopper’s Guide to Pesticides in Produce™
-----------------------------------------------------

EWG has published the Shopper’s Guide to Pesticides in Produce almost every year since 2004.

It’s a suite of materials investigating the presence of pesticides in foods that helps consumers find ways to reduce exposure to pesticides while encouraging them to consume plenty of fruits and vegetables.

It includes two lists. The first is the Clean Fifteen™, the list of conventionally grown fruits and vegetables with very low or no traces of pesticides. The second is the Dirty Dozen™, or the 12 fresh conventional fruits and vegetables with the highest pesticide residues.

Some pesticides have more data linking them to health concerns than others. These pesticides are particularly concerning for children. The brain and nervous systems, as well as other physiological systems of young children, are far from fully developed. They’re especially sensitive to disruption and damage from industrial chemicals, including pesticides.

The presence of so many individual pesticides in food is also concerning from a public health standpoint.

When regulating pesticides, government bodies consider them one at a time, without considering the potential total body burden for consumers. Little available data shows how multiple pesticides interact with each other in the body or how such mixtures could compound each chemical’s individual potential health harms.

But [the data we do have](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29945228/), mostly from animal studies, suggests that chemicals combined in a mixture [can be more toxic than they are individually](https://web.archive.org/web/20260925152914/https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/es0101227).
