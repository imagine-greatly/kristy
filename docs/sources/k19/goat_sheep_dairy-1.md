From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Liquid food produced by female goats

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Liqueur_from_goat_milk%2C_Fuerteventura.JPG/250px-Liqueur_from_goat_milk%2C_Fuerteventura.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Liqueur_from_goat_milk,_Fuerteventura.JPG)

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/64/Geitenmelk.jpg/250px-Geitenmelk.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Geitenmelk.jpg)

**Goat milk** is the [milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk "Milk") of [domestic goats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domestic_goat "Domestic goat"). Goats produce about 2% of the world's total annual milk supply.[[1]](#cite_note-1) Some goats are bred specifically for milk. Goat milk naturally has small, well-emulsified fat globules, which means the cream remains in suspension for a longer period of time than [cow's milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk "Milk"); therefore, it does not need to be [homogenized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogenization_(chemistry) "Homogenization (chemistry)"). Eventually, the cream rises to the top over a period of a few days. If the milk is to be used to make cheese, homogenization is not recommended as this changes the structure of the milk, affecting the culture's ability to coagulate the milk as well as the final quality and yield of the cheese.[[2]](#cite_note-2)

Dairy goats in their prime (generally around the third or fourth lactation cycle) average—2.7 to 3.6 kg (6 to 8 lb)—(or 2.8 to 3.8 L (3 to 4 U.S. qt))—of milk production daily—roughly during a ten-month [lactation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactation "Lactation"). Goats produce more after freshening and gradually drop production toward the end of their lactation. The milk generally averages 3.5% [butterfat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterfat "Butterfat").[[3]](#cite_note-3)

Cheese
------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Goat_milk&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Cheese")]

See also: [List of goat cheeses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_goat_cheeses "List of goat cheeses")

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/78/Moiti%C3%A9_de_p%C3%A9lardon_cendr%C3%A9.jpg/250px-Moiti%C3%A9_de_p%C3%A9lardon_cendr%C3%A9.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Moiti%C3%A9_de_p%C3%A9lardon_cendr%C3%A9.jpg)

[Pélardon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A9lardon "Pélardon") is a type of cheese made from goat milk.

Goat milk is commonly processed into [cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheese "Cheese"), [butter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butter "Butter"), [ice cream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_cream "Ice cream"), [yogurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt "Yogurt"), *[cajeta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulce_de_leche "Dulce de leche")* and other products.

[Goat cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goat_cheese "Goat cheese") is known as *fromage de chèvre* ('goat cheese') in France. Some varieties include [Rocamadour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocamadour_cheese "Rocamadour cheese") and Montrachet.[[4]](#cite_note-4) Goat butter is white because goats produce milk with the yellow [beta-carotene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%CE%92-Carotene "Β-Carotene") converted to a colorless form of [vitamin A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_A "Vitamin A"). Goat milk has less cholesterol.

Nutrition
---------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Goat_milk&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Nutrition")]

A 1970 book on animal husbandry claimed that goat's milk differs from cow’s or human milk in that it has greater digestibility, a different [alkalinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkalinity "Alkalinity"), greater [buffering capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffer_solution#Buffer_capacity "Buffer solution"), and certain therapeutic values in human medicine and nutrition.[[5]](#cite_note-5) George Mateljan suggested that goat’s milk could replace cow’s or [sheep’s milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep's_milk "Sheep's milk") in the diet of those allergic to the milk of certain mammals.[[6]](#cite_note-WHFoods-6) However, like cow’s milk, goat’s milk contains [lactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactose "Lactose") (sugar) and can cause [gastrointestinal problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gastrointestinal_disease "Gastrointestinal disease") for people with [lactose intolerance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactose_intolerance "Lactose intolerance").[[6]](#cite_note-WHFoods-6) In fact, the lactose level is similar to that of cow milk.

Some researchers and companies producing goat dairy products have claimed that goat milk is better for human health than most Western cow's milk because it largely lacks a form of β-casein proteins called A1, and instead contains mostly [the A2 form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A2_milk "A2 milk"), which is not metabolized to [β-casomorphin 7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casomorphin "Casomorphin") in the body.[[7]](#cite_note-7)

A research project at the Department of Physiology of the University of Granada used rats with nutritionally induced iron-deficiency anemia. The research revealed that goat's milk contains properties that help prevent iron-deficiency anemia and bone demineralization. Furthermore, goats’ milk promotes the digestive and metabolic utilization of minerals such as iron, calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium.

### Considerations for infants

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Goat_milk&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Considerations for infants")]

Like whole cow's milk, whole goat's milk is not recommended for use by [infants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infant "Infant") due to the discrepancy between the composition of whole milk and breastmilk and the imperfection of the child's digestive organs for digesting evolutionarily unsuitable food. Lack of [folic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folate "Folate") when using whole milk instead of [breastfeeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breastfeeding "Breastfeeding") in children leads to the development of [B12 folate deficiency anemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megaloblastic_anemia "Megaloblastic anemia"). In the event of an allergic reaction in children to cow's milk, contrary to popular belief, the likelihood of developing it to goat's milk is equally high.

Like unmodified cow's milk, the [American Academy of Pediatrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Pediatrics "American Academy of Pediatrics") (AAP) discourages feeding infants unmodified goat's milk. An April 2010 case report[[10]](#cite_note-10) describes an infant fed raw goat milk and summarizes AAP's recommendation and presents "a comprehensive review of the consequences associated with this dangerous practice." They also voiced, "Many infants are exclusively fed unmodified goat's milk as a result of cultural beliefs as well as exposure to false online information. Anecdotal reports have described a host of morbidities associated with that practice, including severe [electrolyte abnormalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte_imbalance "Electrolyte imbalance"), [metabolic acidosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolic_acidosis "Metabolic acidosis"), [megaloblastic anemia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Megaloblastic_anemia "Megaloblastic anemia"), [allergic reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allergy "Allergy") including life-threatening [anaphylactic shock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaphylaxis#Classification "Anaphylaxis"), [hemolytic–uremic syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemolytic%E2%80%93uremic_syndrome "Hemolytic–uremic syndrome"), and infections." Untreated caprine [brucellosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brucellosis "Brucellosis") results in a 2% case fatality rate. According to the [USDA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Agriculture "United States Department of Agriculture"), doe milk is not recommended for human infants because it contains "inadequate quantities of [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron "Iron"), [folate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folate "Folate"), [vitamins C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_C "Vitamin C") and [D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_D "Vitamin D"), [thiamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiamine "Thiamine"), [niacin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_B3 "Vitamin B3"), [vitamin B6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_B6 "Vitamin B6"), and [pantothenic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantothenic_acid "Pantothenic acid"). Doe milk would not meet an infant's nutritional needs" and may cause harm to an infant's [kidneys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kidney "Kidney") and could cause metabolic damage.

According to the Canadian federal health department *Health Canada*, most of the dangers of, and counter-indications for, feeding unmodified goat's milk to infants parallel those associated with unmodified cow's milk — especially insofar as allergic reactions go.[[11]](#cite_note-11)

### Infant formula

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Goat_milk&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Infant formula")]

Breast milk is the best nutrition for infants. If this is not an option, infant formula is the alternative. EFSA (European Food Safety Association) concluded in 2012 that goat milk protein is suitable as a protein source in infant and follow-on formulas.[[12]](#cite_note-12) Ever since, goat milk-based infant formulas have rapidly gained popularity around the world including: the UK, Australia, Germany, Netherlands, China, Korea, Australia and New Zealand. These formulas are not produced by the infant formula multinationals but by companies that focus on specialty infant formulas. In the U.S. goat milk infant formula is not yet available.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recognizes that goat infant formula has been thoroughly reviewed and supports normal growth and development in infants.[[13]](#cite_note-13)
