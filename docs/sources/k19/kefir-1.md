**Kefir** ([/kəˈfɪər/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English") [*kə-FEER*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Pronunciation_respelling_key "Help:Pronunciation respelling key");[[1]](#cite_note-1)[[2]](#cite_note-2) alternative spellings: **kephir** or **kefier**[[a]](#cite_note-3)) is a [fermented milk drink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermented_milk_products "Fermented milk products") similar to a thin [yogurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt "Yogurt") or [ayran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayran "Ayran") that is made from kefir grains, a specific type of [mesophilic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesophilic "Mesophilic") [symbiotic culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCOBY "SCOBY"). It is prepared by [inoculating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inoculation "Inoculation") the milk of [cows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk#Sources "Milk"), [goats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goat_milk "Goat milk"), or [sheep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheep_milk "Sheep milk") with kefir grains.[[3]](#cite_note-Altay2013-4)

Kefir is a common breakfast, lunch or [dinner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinner "Dinner") drink consumed in countries of [West Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Asia "West Asia") and [Central and Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_and_Eastern_Europe "Central and Eastern Europe"). Kefir is consumed at any time of the day, alongside European pastries like [zelnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zelnik "Zelnik") (zeljanica), [burek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%B6rek "Börek") and [banitsa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banitsa "Banitsa")/[gibanica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibanica "Gibanica"), and is an ingredient in cold soups.

Origin and etymology
--------------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kefir&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Origin and etymology")]

Kefir has been found in graves in the Bronze Age [Xiaohe Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xiaohe_Cemetery "Xiaohe Cemetery"), dating back 3,600 years.[[4]](#cite_note-5)

The word *kefir* is of North Caucasian origin, where related words are attested in several languages: [Mingrelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mingrelian_language "Mingrelian language") ქიფური (*kipuri*), [Karachay-Balkar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karachay-Balkar "Karachay-Balkar") гыпы (*gıpı*) and [Ossetian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ossetian_language "Ossetian language") къӕпы (*k'æpy*).[[5]](#cite_note-Shansky-6)[[6]](#cite_note-Chernyx-7) One theory is that the word comes from [Old Turkic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Turkic "Old Turkic") *köpür* '(milk) froth'.[[7]](#cite_note-ahd-8) It was loaned into Russian where it is attested since 1870s,[[6]](#cite_note-Chernyx-7) and from there it was loaned into English, there attested in 1880s.[[8]](#cite_note-9) It has become an [internationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internationalism_(linguistics) "Internationalism (linguistics)"), as it is found in many European languages.[[6]](#cite_note-Chernyx-7)

Kefir is a very popular drink in the North Caucasus, it is a part of traditional [Circassian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circassian_cuisine "Circassian cuisine") to this day.[*[improper synthesis?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:No_original_research#Synthesis_of_published_material "Wikipedia:No original research")*][[9]](#cite_note-10)[[10]](#cite_note-11)[[11]](#cite_note-12)[[12]](#cite_note-13)[[13]](#cite_note-14) Traditional kefir was made in [goatskin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goatskin_(material) "Goatskin (material)") bags that were hung near a doorway; the bags would be knocked by anyone passing through to keep the milk and kefir grains well mixed.[[14]](#cite_note-15) In Karachay-Balkar, **gıpı** has a connection with **gıpıt** ([wineskin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wineskin "Wineskin")). It was under the name *wineskin* that Karachay kefir was distributed in the second half of the 19th century and at the beginning of the 20th century.[[15]](#cite_note-16)[[16]](#cite_note-17) Kefir spread from the former Soviet Union to the rest of Europe, Canada, Japan, and the United States by the early 21st century.[[17]](#cite_note-18)[[18]](#cite_note-arslan-19)

Fermentation and kefir grain formation
--------------------------------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kefir&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Fermentation and kefir grain formation")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/Kefirpilze.jpg/250px-Kefirpilze.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Kefirpilze.jpg)

Kefir grains, a matrix of bacteria and yeasts

Traditional kefir is fermented at ambient temperatures, generally overnight. Fermentation of the lactose yields a [sour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taste#Sourness "Taste"), [carbonated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonation "Carbonation"), slightly alcoholic beverage, with a consistency and taste similar to drinkable [yogurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt "Yogurt").[[19]](#cite_note-kowsikoski-20)

The kefir grains initiating the fermentation are initially created by auto-aggregations of *[Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus_kefiranofaciens "Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens")* and *Maudiozyma turicensis*[[20]](#cite_note-21) or *[M. humilis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maudiozyma_humilis "Maudiozyma humilis")*,[[21]](#cite_note-22) where multiple [biofilm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biofilm "Biofilm") producers cause the surfaces to adhere which form a three dimensional microcolony.[[22]](#cite_note-23)[[23]](#cite_note-24)[[24]](#cite_note-25) The biofilm is a [matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_(biology)#Matrix_(medium) "Matrix (biology)") of [heteropolysaccharides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysaccharide "Polysaccharide") called kefiran, which is composed of equal proportions of [glucose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucose "Glucose") and [galactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galactose "Galactose").[[25]](#cite_note-Prado-26) It resembles small [cauliflower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauliflower "Cauliflower") grains, with color ranging from white to creamy yellow. A complex and highly variable symbiotic community can be found in these grains, which can include [acetic acid bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetic_acid_bacteria "Acetic acid bacteria") (such as *[Acetobacter aceti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetobacter_aceti "Acetobacter aceti")* and *Acetobacter rasens*), yeasts (such as *[Kluyveromyces lactis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kluyveromyces_lactis "Kluyveromyces lactis")*, [*K. marxianus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kluyveromyces_marxianus "Kluyveromyces marxianus"), *[Saccharomyces cerevisiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccharomyces_cerevisiae "Saccharomyces cerevisiae")*, *M. turicensis*) and a number of *Lactobacillus* species, such as [*L. parakefiri*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus_parakefiri "Lactobacillus parakefiri"), *L. kefiranofaciens* (and subsp. *kefirgranum*[[26]](#cite_note-27)), [*L. kefiri*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lentilactobacillus_kefiri "Lentilactobacillus kefiri"),[[27]](#cite_note-28)[*L. brevis*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levilactobacillus_brevis "Levilactobacillus brevis"),[[28]](#cite_note-29) etc.[[25]](#cite_note-Prado-26) While some microbes predominate, *[Lactobacillus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus "Lactobacillus")* species are always present.[[29]](#cite_note-Oliveira2013-30) The microbe flora can vary between batches of kefir due to factors such as the kefir grains rising out of the milk while fermenting or curds forming around the grains, as well as temperature.[[30]](#cite_note-31) Additionally, Tibetan kefir composition differs from that of the Russian kefir, Irish kefir, Taiwan kefir and Turkey [fermented beverage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermented_beverage "Fermented beverage") with kefir.[[25]](#cite_note-Prado-26) In recent years, the use of [freeze-dried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeze-drying "Freeze-drying") starter culture has become common due to stability of the fermentation result, because the species of microbes are selected in laboratory conditions, as well as easy transportation.[[31]](#cite_note-32)[[32]](#cite_note-33)[[33]](#cite_note-34)

During fermentation, changes in the composition of ingredients occur. [Lactose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactose "Lactose"), the sugar present in milk, is broken down mostly to [lactic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactic_acid "Lactic acid") by the lactic acid bacteria, which results in acidification.[[29]](#cite_note-Oliveira2013-30)[Propionibacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propionibacteria "Propionibacteria") further break down some of the lactic acid into [propionic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propionic_acid "Propionic acid") (these bacteria also carry out the same fermentation in [Swiss cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Swiss_cheeses "List of Swiss cheeses")). Other substances that contribute to the flavor of kefir are [pyruvic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyruvic_acid "Pyruvic acid"), [acetic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetic_acid "Acetic acid"), [diacetyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diacetyl "Diacetyl") and [acetoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetoin "Acetoin") (both of which contribute a "buttery" flavor), [citric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citric_acid "Citric acid"), [acetaldehyde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acetaldehyde "Acetaldehyde"), and [amino acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amino_acids "Amino acids") resulting from protein breakdown.[[34]](#cite_note-Van_Wyk_2019-35)

### Decreased lactose content

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kefir&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Decreased lactose content")]

During the fermentation process, bacteria and yeast break lactose down into glucose and galactose.[[35]](#cite_note-36) As a result of the fermentation, lactose levels are decreased by 20–30% with respect to the initial lactose levels present in the milk.[[36]](#cite_note-37) One study found that when people with [lactose intolerance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactose_intolerance "Lactose intolerance") consumed the same amount of lactose in milk, kefir or [yogurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt "Yogurt") products, the latter two showed significantly reduced symptoms of lactose intolerance during the first 8 hours after consumption.[[37]](#cite_note-38) This result suggests that yogurt and kefir might be suitable for people with lactose intolerance. However, the long-term impact of kefir consumption on lactose intolerance has not been studied. It has also been shown that fermented milk products have a slower transit time than milk, which may further improve lactose digestion.[[38]](#cite_note-Farnworth2005-39)

### Alcohol content

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kefir&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Alcohol content")]

Kefir contains a small amount of [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol "Ethanol").[[39]](#cite_note-Laur-40) The level of ethanol in kefir can vary by production method. A 2016 study of kefir sold in Germany showed an ethanol level of only 0.02 g per litre, which was attributed to fermentation under controlled conditions allowing the growth of *Lactobacteria* only, but excluding the growth of other microorganisms that generate much higher amounts of ethanol.[[40]](#cite_note-Gorgus-41) A 2008 study of German commercial kefir found levels of 0.002–0.005% of ethanol.[[41]](#cite_note-Farnworth-42) Kefir produced by small-scale dairies in Russia in the early 20th century had 1–2% ethanol,[[41]](#cite_note-Farnworth-42) but this is no longer common.

Nutrition
---------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kefir&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Nutrition")]

Plain kefir is 87% water, 7% [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate "Carbohydrate"), 4% [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein_(nutrient) "Protein (nutrient)"), and 1% [fat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fat "Fat") (table). In a reference amount of 100 g (3.5 oz), kefir provides 52 [calories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calorie "Calorie") of [food energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_energy "Food energy"), and contains moderate amounts (10-19% of the [Daily Value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_Value "Daily Value"), DV) of [vitamin A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_A "Vitamin A"), [vitamin B12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_B12 "Vitamin B12"), [riboflavin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riboflavin "Riboflavin"), and [calcium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium_in_biology "Calcium in biology") (table).

Kefir contains byproducts of the fermentation process, including [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") and [ethanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethanol "Ethanol").[[44]](#cite_note-Ahmed2013-45)[*[better source needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability#Questionable_sources "Wikipedia:Verifiability")*]

### Microbiota

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kefir&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Microbiota")]

[Probiotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probiotic "Probiotic") bacteria found in kefir products include *[Lactobacillus acidophilus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus_acidophilus "Lactobacillus acidophilus")*, *[Bifidobacterium bifidum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bifidobacterium_bifidum "Bifidobacterium bifidum")*, *[Streptococcus thermophilus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streptococcus_thermophilus "Streptococcus thermophilus")*, *[Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus_delbrueckii_subsp._bulgaricus "Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus")*, *[Lactobacillus helveticus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus_helveticus "Lactobacillus helveticus")*, *[Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus_kefiranofaciens "Lactobacillus kefiranofaciens")*, *[Lactococcus lactis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactococcus_lactis "Lactococcus lactis")*, and *[Leuconostoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leuconostoc "Leuconostoc")* species.[[29]](#cite_note-Oliveira2013-30)[[45]](#cite_note-Guzel2011-46)[[38]](#cite_note-Farnworth2005-39)[Lactobacilli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacilli "Lactobacilli") in kefir may exist in concentrations varying from approximately 1 million to 1 billion [colony-forming units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colony-forming_unit "Colony-forming unit") per milliliter, and are the bacteria responsible for the synthesis of the polysaccharide *kefiran*.[[3]](#cite_note-Altay2013-4)

In addition to bacteria, kefir often contains strains of [yeast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeast "Yeast") that can metabolize lactose, such as *[Kluyveromyces marxianus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kluyveromyces_marxianus "Kluyveromyces marxianus")*, *[Kluyveromyces lactis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kluyveromyces_lactis "Kluyveromyces lactis")*, and *[Saccharomyces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccharomyces "Saccharomyces") fragilis*, as well as strains of yeast that do not metabolize lactose, including *[Saccharomyces cerevisiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccharomyces_cerevisiae "Saccharomyces cerevisiae")*, *[Torulaspora delbrueckii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torulaspora_delbrueckii "Torulaspora delbrueckii")*, and *Kazachstania unispora*.[[29]](#cite_note-Oliveira2013-30) The nutritional significance of these strains is not currently known with certainty.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/25/Kefir-insieme.jpg/250px-Kefir-insieme.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Kefir-insieme.jpg)

Kefir production

Modern kefir is made by adding kefir grains to milk typically at a proportion of 2–5% grains-to-milk. The mixture is then placed in a corrosion-resistant container, such as a glass jar, and stored preferably in the dark to prevent degradation of light-sensitive vitamins. After a period between 12 and 24 hours of fermentation at mild temperature, ideally 20–25 °C (68–77 °F),[[34]](#cite_note-Van_Wyk_2019-35) the grains are strained from the milk using a corrosion-resistant (stainless steel or plastic) utensil and kept to produce another batch. During the fermentation process the grains enlarge and eventually split forming new units.

The resulting fermented liquid may be drunk, used in recipes, or kept aside in a sealed container for additional time to undergo a secondary fermentation. Because of its acidity the beverage should not be stored in reactive metal containers such as aluminium, copper, or zinc, as these may leach into it over time. The shelf life, unrefrigerated, is up to thirty days.[[46]](#cite_note-47)

The Russian method permits production of kefir on a larger scale and uses two fermentations. The first step is to prepare the cultures by inoculating milk with 2–3% grains as described. The grains are then removed by filtration and 1–3% of the resulting liquid mother culture is added to milk and fermented for 12 to 18 hours.[[47]](#cite_note-48)

Kefir can be made using [freeze-dried](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freeze-drying "Freeze-drying") cultures commonly available in powder form from [health food stores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_food_store "Health food store"). A portion of the resulting kefir can be saved to be used a number of times to propagate further fermentations but ultimately does not form grains.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In Taiwan, researchers were able to produce kefir in a laboratory using microorganisms isolated from kefir grains. They report that the resulting kefir drink had chemical properties similar to homemade kefir.[[48]](#cite_note-Chen-49)

Milk types
----------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kefir&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Milk types")]

Kefir grains will ferment the milk from most [mammals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mammals "Mammals") and will continue to grow in such milk. Typical animal milks used include cow, goat, and sheep, each with varying [organoleptic](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/organoleptic "wikt:organoleptic") (flavor, aroma, and texture) and nutritional qualities. [Raw milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raw_milk "Raw milk") has been traditionally used.

[Milk sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk_sugar "Milk sugar") is not essential for the synthesis of the [polysaccharide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysaccharide "Polysaccharide") that makes up the grains (kefiran), and rice hydrolysate is a suitable alternative medium.[[49]](#cite_note-maeda1-50) Additionally, kefir grains will reproduce when fermenting soy milk, although they will change in appearance and size due to the differing proteins available to them.[[50]](#cite_note-51)

A different type of [SCOBY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCOBY "SCOBY") that thrives in sugary water also exists, called [water kefir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibicos "Tibicos") (or *tibicos*), and can vary markedly from milk kefir in both appearance and microbial composition.[[51]](#cite_note-52)

Culinary
--------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kefir&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Culinary")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/Chlodnik_%28Cold_Borscht%29.jpg/250px-Chlodnik_%28Cold_Borscht%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Chlodnik_(Cold_Borscht).jpg)

The [Lithuanian soup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_cuisine "Lithuanian cuisine") *šaltibarščiai*, also known as "cold [borscht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borscht "Borscht")", consumed in Eastern European countries

As it contains *Lactobacillus* bacteria, kefir can be used to make a [sourdough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sourdough "Sourdough") [starter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermentation_starter "Fermentation starter").[[52]](#cite_note-53) It is also useful as a [buttermilk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buttermilk "Buttermilk") substitute in [baking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baking "Baking"). Kefir is one of the main ingredients in [cold borscht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_beet_soup "Cold beet soup") soup in [Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_cuisine "Lithuanian cuisine"), also known in Poland as *Lithuanian cold soup* (*chłodnik litewski*), and other countries. The kefir-based soup [okroshka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okroshka "Okroshka") is common across the former [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_cuisine "Soviet cuisine"). Kefir may be used in place of milk on cereal, [granola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granola "Granola"), [milkshakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milkshakes "Milkshakes"), [salad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salad "Salad") dressing, [ice cream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_cream "Ice cream"), [smoothies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoothies "Smoothies") and [soup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soup "Soup").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In [Estonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonia "Estonia"), kefir is also often mixed with [kama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kama_(food) "Kama (food)") and eaten as a snack or a quick breakfast.[[53]](#cite_note-54)
