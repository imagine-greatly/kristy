**Kimchi** ([/ˈkɪmtʃi/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); [Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language"): 김치; pronounced [[kim.tɕʰi]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Korean "Help:IPA/Korean"))[[a]](#cite_note-fn1-1) is a traditional Korean side dish (*[banchan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banchan "Banchan")*) consisting of salted and [fermented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermentation_in_food_processing "Fermentation in food processing") vegetables, most often [Chinese cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napa_cabbage "Napa cabbage") or [Korean radish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_radish "Korean radish"). A wide selection of seasonings are used, including *[gochugaru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gochugaru "Gochugaru")* (Korean chili powder), [spring onions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_onion "Spring onion"), [garlic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garlic "Garlic"), [ginger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginger "Ginger"), and *[jeotgal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeotgal "Jeotgal")* (salted seafood).[[1]](#cite_note-Britannica-2)[[2]](#cite_note-Chin-3) Kimchi is also used in a variety of soups and stews. Kimchi is a [staple food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staple_food "Staple food") in [Korean cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_cuisine "Korean cuisine") and is eaten as a side dish with almost every Korean meal.[[3]](#cite_note-:5-4)

There are hundreds of different types of kimchi made with different vegetables as the main ingredients.[[2]](#cite_note-Chin-3) Examples of variants include [*baechu-kimchi*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baechu-kimchi "Baechu-kimchi"), *[kkakdugi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kkakdugi "Kkakdugi")*, *chonggak-kimchi*, and *oi-sobagi*. Traditionally, winter kimchi, called *[gimjang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimjang "Gimjang")*, was stored in large earthenware fermentation vessels, called *[onggi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onggi "Onggi")*, in the ground to prevent freezing during the winter months and to keep it cool enough to slow down the fermentation process during summer months.[[4]](#cite_note-:6-5) The process of making kimchi was called gimjang and was based on participation by the whole village. The vessels are also kept outdoors in special terraces called *[jangdokdae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jangdokdae "Jangdokdae")*. The use of household [kimchi refrigerators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimchi_refrigerator "Kimchi refrigerator") has become a more common method for keeping fermenting kimchi at the right temperature.[[2]](#cite_note-Chin-3)

Kimchi has gained international recognition as a traditional Korean food and has been promoted as part of Korean cultural heritage. Its preparation methods and cultural significance have been documented as part of Korea's intangible cultural heritage.[[5]](#cite_note-6)

Etymology
---------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Etymology")]

### *Ji*

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Ji")]

The term *ji* (지), which has its origins in archaic Korean *dihi* (디히), has been used to refer to kimchi since ancient times.[[6]](#cite_note-Lee-7) The [sound change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_change "Sound change") can be roughly described as:[[7]](#cite_note-Hong_G-8)

* *dihi* (디히) → *di* (디) → *ji* (지)

The [Middle Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Korean "Middle Korean") form *dihi* is found in several books from the [Joseon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseon "Joseon") period (1392–1897).[[8]](#cite_note-Uichim-9)[[9]](#cite_note-Sin-10) In [Modern Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language"), the word remains as the suffix *-ji* in the [standard language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korean_standard_language "South Korean standard language") (as in *[jjanji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jjanji "Jjanji")*, *seokbak-ji*),[[10]](#cite_note-SKLD_jjan-11)[[11]](#cite_note-SKLD_seokbak-12) and as the suffix *-ji* as well as the noun *ji* in [Gyeongsang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyeongsang_dialect "Gyeongsang dialect") and [Jeolla dialects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeolla_dialect "Jeolla dialect").[[12]](#cite_note-SKLD_ji-13) The [unpalatalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatalization_(phonetics) "Palatalization (phonetics)") form *di* is preserved in [P'yŏngan dialect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyongan_dialect "Pyongan dialect").[[13]](#cite_note-SKLD_di-14)

### *Kimchi*

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Kimchi")]

*Kimchi* (김치) is the accepted word in both [North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Korean_standard_language "North Korean standard language") and [South Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Korean_standard_language "South Korean standard language") standard languages. Earlier forms of the word include *thimchoy* (팀ᄎᆡ), a [Middle Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Korean "Middle Korean") transcription of the [Sino-Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Korean_vocabulary "Sino-Korean vocabulary") word [沈](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E6%B2%88 "wikt:沈")[菜](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E8%8F%9C "wikt:菜") (literally "submerged vegetable"). *Thimchoy* appears in *Sohak Eonhae*,[[14]](#cite_note-Jeong_G-15) the 16th-century Korean rendition of the Chinese book, *Xiaoxue*.[[15]](#cite_note-Zhu-16) Sound changes from Middle Korean to Modern Korean regarding the word can be described as:[[16]](#cite_note-SKLD-17)

* *thimchoy* (팀ᄎᆡ; 沈菜) → *timchoy* (딤ᄎᆡ) → *cimchoy* (짐ᄎᆡ) → *cimchuy* (짐츼) → *kimchi* (김치)

The [aspirated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspirated_consonant "Aspirated consonant") first consonant of *thimchoy* became unaspirated in *timchoy*, then underwent [palatalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palatalization_(sound_change) "Palatalization (sound change)") in *cimchoy*. The word then became *cimchuy* with the loss of the vowel *o* (ㆍ) in Korean language, then *kimchi*, with the depalatalized word-initial consonant. In Modern Korean, the [hanja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanja "Hanja") characters 沈菜 are pronounced *chimchae* (침채), and are not used to refer to kimchi, or anything else. The word *kimchi* is not considered as a [Sino-Korean word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Korean_vocabulary "Sino-Korean vocabulary").[[16]](#cite_note-SKLD-17) Older forms of the word are retained in many regional dialects: *jimchae* (Jeolla, [Hamgyŏng dialects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamgy%C5%8Fng_dialect "Hamgyŏng dialect")),[[17]](#cite_note-SKLD_jimchae-18)*jimchi* ([Chungcheong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chungcheong_dialect "Chungcheong dialect"), [Gangwon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gangwon_dialect "Gangwon dialect"), [Gyeonggi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyeonggi_dialect "Gyeonggi dialect"), Gyeongsang, Hamgyŏng, Jeolla dialects),[[18]](#cite_note-SKLD_jimchi-19) and *dimchi* (P'yŏngan dialect).[[19]](#cite_note-SKLD_dimchi-20)

The spelling "kimchi" originated from the [McCune–Reischauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McCune%E2%80%93Reischauer "McCune–Reischauer") transcription *kimch'i* (김치).

History
-------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: History")]

### Early history

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Early history")]

*[Samguk Sagi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samguk_Sagi "Samguk Sagi")*, a historical record of the [Three Kingdoms of Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Kingdoms_of_Korea "Three Kingdoms of Korea"), mentions the [pickle jar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onggi "Onggi") used to ferment vegetables, which indicates that fermented vegetables were commonly eaten during this time.[[20]](#cite_note-Hui-21)[[21]](#cite_note-Gim-22) Attributed to the earliest kimchi, the [Goguryeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goguryeo "Goguryeo") people were skilled at fermenting and widely consumed fermented food.[[22]](#cite_note-23) During the [Silla dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silla_dynasty "Silla dynasty") (57 BCE – CE 935), kimchi became prevalent as [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism") caught on throughout the nation and fostered a vegetarian lifestyle.[[23]](#cite_note-btpickle-201309-24)

The pickling of vegetables was an ideal method, prior to refrigerators, that helped to preserve the lifespan of foods. In Korea, kimchi was made during the winter by fermenting vegetables, and burying them in the ground in traditional brown ceramic pots called *[onggi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onggi "Onggi")*. This labor further allowed a bonding among women within the family.[[23]](#cite_note-btpickle-201309-24) A poem on [Korean radish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_radish "Korean radish") written by Yi Gyubo, a 13th-century literatus, shows that radish kimchi was common in [Goryeo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goryeo "Goryeo") (918–1392).[[24]](#cite_note-Pettid-25)[[25]](#cite_note-Yi_G-26)[[26]](#cite_note-Breidt-27)

> Pickled radish slices make a good summer side dish,  
> Radish preserved in salt is a winter side dish from start to end.  
> The roots in the earth grow plumper every day,  
> Harvesting after the frost, a slice cut by a knife tastes like a pear.

— Yi Gyubo, *[Donggukisanggukjip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donggukisanggukjip "Donggukisanggukjip")* (translated by Michael J. Pettid, in *Korean cuisine: An Illustrated History*)

Kimchi has been a staple in Korean culture, but historical versions were not a spicy dish.[[27]](#cite_note-28) Early records of kimchi do not mention garlic or [chili pepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chili_pepper "Chili pepper").[[28]](#cite_note-KCIS-29) Chili peppers, now a standard ingredient in kimchi, had been unknown in Korea until the early seventeenth century due to it being a [New World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World "New World") crop.[[29]](#cite_note-30) Chili peppers, originally native to the Americas, were introduced to East Asia by Portuguese traders.[[28]](#cite_note-KCIS-29)[[30]](#cite_note-Park-31)[[31]](#cite_note-Marianski-32) The first mention of chili pepper is found in *[Jibong yuseol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jibong_yuseol "Jibong yuseol")*, an encyclopedia published in 1614.[[20]](#cite_note-Hui-21)[[32]](#cite_note-Yi_S-33)*[Sallim gyeongje](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sallim_gyeongje "Sallim gyeongje")*, a 17‒18th century book on farm management, wrote on kimchi with chili peppers.[[20]](#cite_note-Hui-21)[[33]](#cite_note-Hong_M-34) However, it was not until the 19th century that the use of chili peppers in kimchi became widespread.[[34]](#cite_note-Cho-35) Recipes from the early 19th century closely resemble today's kimchi.[[35]](#cite_note-Jeong_H-36)[[36]](#cite_note-Hong_S-37)

A 1766 book, *[Jeungbo sallim gyeongje](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeungbo_sallim_gyeongje "Jeungbo sallim gyeongje")*, reports kimchi varieties made with myriad ingredients, including *chonggak-kimchi* (kimchi made with [chonggak radish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chonggak_radish "Chonggak radish")), *oi-sobagi* (with cucumber), *seokbak-ji* (with *[jogi-jeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jogi-jeot "Jogi-jeot")*), and *[dongchimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dongchimi "Dongchimi")*.[[20]](#cite_note-Hui-21)[[37]](#cite_note-Yu-38) However, [napa cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napa_cabbage "Napa cabbage") was introduced to Korea only at the end of 19th century,[[34]](#cite_note-Cho-35) and whole-cabbage kimchi similar to its current form is described in *[Siuijeonseo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siuijeonseo "Siuijeonseo")*, a cookbook published around that time.[[38]](#cite_note-Sim-39)

### Modern history

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Modern history")]

During South Korea's involvement in the [Vietnam War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_War "Vietnam War"), the industrialization and commercialization of kimchi production became increasingly important because the Korean government wanted to provide [rations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_ration "Field ration") for its [troops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Korea_Army "Republic of Korea Army"). The Korean government requested American help to ensure that South Korean troops, reportedly "desperate" for the food, could obtain it in the field.[[39]](#cite_note-Choe-40)[[40]](#cite_note-:8-41)

In 2008, South Korean scientists created a special low-calorie, vitamin-rich "space kimchi" for [Yi So-yeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yi_So-yeon "Yi So-yeon"), the first Korean astronaut, to take to space. It was bacteria-free, unlike normal kimchi in which bacteria are essential for fermentation. It was feared that cosmic rays might mutate the bacteria.[[41]](#cite_note-History_of_Kimchi,_the_Korean_soul_food._Kimchi_fun_facts_we_BAECHU_didn't_know.-42)

South Korea developed programs for adult [Korean adoptees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_adoption_of_South_Korean_children "International adoption of South Korean children") to return to South Korea and learn about what it means to be Korean. One of these programs was learning how to make kimchi.[[42]](#cite_note-43)

#### 1996 kimchi standard dispute with Japan

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: 1996 kimchi standard dispute with Japan")]

In 1996, Korea protested against Japanese commercial production of kimchi arguing that the Japanese-produced product (*[kimuchi](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/kimuchi "wikt:kimuchi")*, [キムチ](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E3%82%AD%E3%83%A0%E3%83%81 "wikt:キムチ")) was different from kimchi. In particular, Japanese kimchi was not fermented and was more similar to [asazuke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asazuke "Asazuke"). Korea lobbied for an international standard from the [Codex Alimentarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Alimentarius "Codex Alimentarius"), an organization associated with the World Health Organization that defines voluntary standards for food preparation for international trade purposes.[[43]](#cite_note-LAT-44)[[44]](#cite_note-NYT-45) In 2001, the Codex Alimentarius published a voluntary standard defining kimchi as "a fermented food that uses salted napa cabbages as its main ingredient mixed with seasonings, and goes through a lactic acid production process at a low temperature", but which neither specified a minimum amount of fermentation nor forbade the use of any additives.[[45]](#cite_note-46) Following the inclusion of the kimchi standard, kimchi exports in Korea did increase, but so did the production of kimchi in China and the import of Chinese kimchi into Korea.[[40]](#cite_note-:8-41)

#### 2010 kimchi ingredient price crisis

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: 2010 kimchi ingredient price crisis")]

Due to heavy rainfall shortening the harvesting time for cabbage and other main ingredients for kimchi in 2010, the price of kimchi ingredients and kimchi itself rose greatly. Korean and international newspapers described the rise in prices as a national crisis.[[46]](#cite_note-EconomistOct72010-47) Some restaurants stopped offering kimchi as a free side dish, which *The New York Times* compared to an American hamburger restaurant no longer offering free [ketchup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketchup "Ketchup").[[47]](#cite_note-nyt2010-48) In response to the kimchi price crisis, the South Korean government announced the temporary reduction of tariffs on imported cabbage to coincide with the [kimjang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimjang "Gimjang") season.[[46]](#cite_note-EconomistOct72010-47)

#### Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity")]

Kimchi-related items have been inscribed on [UNESCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UNESCO "UNESCO")'s [Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_List_of_the_Intangible_Cultural_Heritage_of_Humanity "Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity") by both South and North Korea. This makes kimchi the second intangible heritage that was submitted by two countries, the other one being the folk song "[Arirang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arirang "Arirang")" which was also submitted by both North and South Korea.[[48]](#cite_note-worl_UNES-49) "The culture of kimjang" was the subject of the Intangible Cultural Heritage: kimchi is not registered by itself.

##### Submitted by South Korea (inscribed 2013)

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Submitted by South Korea (inscribed 2013)")]

[Kimjang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimjang "Gimjang"), the tradition of making and sharing kimchi that usually takes place in late autumn, was added to the list as "Gimjang, making and sharing kimchi in the Republic of Korea". The practice of Gimjang reaffirms Korean identity and strengthens family cooperation. Gimjang is also an important reminder for many Koreans that human communities need to live in harmony with nature.[[49]](#cite_note-50)

##### Submitted by North Korea (inscribed 2015)

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Submitted by North Korea (inscribed 2015)")]

North Korean kimchi-making was inscribed on the list in December 2015[[48]](#cite_note-worl_UNES-49) as "Tradition of kimchi-making in the Democratic People's Republic of Korea".[[50]](#cite_note-unes_Trad-51) North Korean kimchi tends to be less spicy and less red than South Korean kimchi.[[51]](#cite_note-yout_Nort-52) Seafood is used less often and less salt is added. Additional sugar is used to help with fermentation in the cold climate.[[52]](#cite_note-53)

#### 2012 effective ban by China of Korean kimchi imports

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: 2012 effective ban by China of Korean kimchi imports")]

Since 2012, the Chinese government has effectively banned the import of Korean kimchi through government regulations. Ignoring the standards of kimchi outlined by the *Codex Alimentarius*, China defined kimchi as a derivative of one of its own cuisines, called *[pao cai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pao_cai "Pao cai")*.[[55]](#cite_note-seoul.co.kr-56) However, due to significantly different preparation techniques from *pao cai*, kimchi has significantly more lactic acid bacteria through its fermentation process, which exceeds China's regulations.[[56]](#cite_note-57) Since 2012, commercial exports of Korean kimchi to China has reached zero; the only minor amounts of exports accounting for Korean kimchi are exhibition events held in China.[[55]](#cite_note-seoul.co.kr-56)

#### 2017 boycott in China

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: 2017 boycott in China")]

A 2017 article in *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* said that [anti-Korean sentiment in China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Korean_sentiment_in_China "Anti-Korean sentiment in China") had risen after South Korea's acceptance of the [deployment of THAAD in South Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China%E2%80%93South_Korea_relations#Deployment_of_THAAD_in_South_Korea "China–South Korea relations"). Government-run Chinese news media encouraged the boycott of South Korean goods, and some Chinese nationalists vowed not to eat kimchi.[[57]](#cite_note-58) The move was criticized by other Chinese nationalists, who noted that China officially considered Koreans an integral ethnic group in the multinational state, and that kimchi is also indigenous to the [Yanbian Korean Autonomous Prefecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yanbian_Korean_Autonomous_Prefecture "Yanbian Korean Autonomous Prefecture").[[58]](#cite_note-kimchi-yanbian-59)

#### 2020 kimchi ISO standard dispute with China

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: 2020 kimchi ISO standard dispute with China")]

In November 2020, the [International Organization for Standardization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Organization_for_Standardization "International Organization for Standardization") (ISO) posted ISO 24220:2020, new regulations for the making of *[pao cai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pao_cai "Pao cai")*.[[59]](#cite_note-60) The same month, [BBC News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBC_News "BBC News") reported that Chinese news organization *[Global Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Times "Global Times")* claimed the new ISO standard was "an international standard for the kimchi industry led by China" despite the standard clearly stating "this document does not apply to kimchi".[[60]](#cite_note-BBC2020-11-30-61) This sparked strong anger from South Korean media and people,[[61]](#cite_note-62) as well as the responses from some Chinese people who argued China held the right to claim kimchi as their own.[[62]](#cite_note-63)

However clarifications from both countries, later revealed that the controversy was triggered over a misunderstanding of a translation of the Chinese word *pao cai*.[[63]](#cite_note-64) After the controversy emerged, *Global Times* explained it was simply a "misunderstanding in translation", where they had meant to refer to Chinese *pao cai*, and their Chinese language article had used the term *pao cai*, but their English language version had "erroneously" translated it as "kimchi", and that the dispute arose from being innocently "lost in translation".[[64]](#cite_note-:7-65)[[65]](#cite_note-66) They acknowledged that kimchi and *pao cai* are two different foods, where "Kimchi refers to a kind of fermented cabbage dish that plays an integral role in Korean cuisine, while *pàocài*, or Sichuan *pàocài*, refers to pickled vegetables that are popular originally in Southwest China's Sichuan Province, but now in most parts of northern China."[[64]](#cite_note-:7-65)*Global Times* also reported that [Baidu Baike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baidu_Baike "Baidu Baike"), a Chinese online encyclopedia, removed the controversial phrase "Korean kimchi originated from China" after the request.[[64]](#cite_note-:7-65)

According to Sojin Lim, co-director of the Institute of Korean Studies of the [University of Central Lancashire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Central_Lancashire "University of Central Lancashire"), Korean kimchi is often called *pao cai* in China, but China has its own [Sichuanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sichuan_cuisine "Sichuan cuisine") fermented vegetable dish that it also calls *pao cai*.[[66]](#cite_note-67) In 2021, the South Korean [Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Culture,_Sports_and_Tourism_(South_Korea) "Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism (South Korea)") subsequently presented the guidelines to set the term *xīnqí* (辛奇) as the new proper Chinese translation of kimchi, while *pàocài* was no longer the acceptable translation.[[67]](#cite_note-68) However, [CNN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNN "CNN") reported that the new Chinese translation of kimchi was unpopular with both Chinese and Korean netizens, and that some Chinese people complained that they do recognize the difference between dishes, but do not like to be told how to translate Kimchi in Chinese. There were also complaints among Koreans that Korea is appropriating their own traditional culture for the Chinese, by trying to promote a Chinese term for Kimchi which does not have an authentic Korean sound.[[68]](#cite_note-69)

### Seasonings

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Seasonings")]

[Brining salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brining_salt "Brining salt") (with a larger grain size compared to [kitchen salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitchen_salt "Kitchen salt")) is used mainly for initial salting of kimchi vegetables. Being minimally processed, it serves to help develop flavors in fermented foods.[[70]](#cite_note-71) Cabbage is usually salted twice when making spicy kimchi.

Commonly used seasonings include *[gochugaru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gochugaru "Gochugaru")* (chili powder), [scallions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scallion "Scallion"), [garlic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garlic "Garlic"), [ginger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ginger "Ginger"), and *[jeotgal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeotgal "Jeotgal")* (salted seafood)[[1]](#cite_note-Britannica-2)[[2]](#cite_note-Chin-3)*Jeotgal* can be replaced with raw seafood in colder Northern parts of the [Korean Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_Peninsula "Korean Peninsula").[[71]](#cite_note-Doo-72) If used, milder *[saeu-jeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saeu-jeot "Saeu-jeot")* (salted shrimp) or *[jogi-jeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jogi-jeot "Jogi-jeot")* (salted croaker) is preferred and the amount of *jeotgal* is also reduced in Northern and Central regions.[[71]](#cite_note-Doo-72) In Southern Korea, on the other hand, a generous amount of stronger *[myeolchi-jeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myeolchi-jeot "Myeolchi-jeot")* (salted anchovies) and *[galchi-jeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galchi-jeot "Galchi-jeot")* (salted [hairtail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichiurus_lepturus "Trichiurus lepturus")) is commonly used.[[71]](#cite_note-Doo-72) Raw seafood or *[daegu-agami-jeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daegu-agami-jeot "Daegu-agami-jeot")* (salted cod gills) are used in the East coast areas.[[71]](#cite_note-Doo-72)

Salt, scallions, garlic, fish sauce, and sugar are commonly added to flavor the kimchi.[[72]](#cite_note-73)

Production
----------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Production")]

To make kimchi, cabbage or daikon is sliced into small, uniform pieces to increase surface area. The pieces are then coated with salt to draw out water and increase salt content, which helps preserve them by preventing the growth of harmful microorganisms. This salting process can use 5–7% salt for 12 hours or 15% salt for 3–7 hours.

After salting, excess water is drained and the seasoning ingredients are added. Sugar can be used to bind any remaining water. Finally, the brined vegetables are packed into airtight jars and left to ferment at room temperature for 24 to 48 hours. The ideal salt concentration during fermentation is about 3%.

Since the fermentation process results in the production of carbon dioxide, the jar should be "burped" daily to release the gas.[[73]](#cite_note-:3-74) The more fermentation that occurs, the more carbon dioxide will be incorporated, which results in a very carbonated-drink-like effect.

In early fermentation stages, the *[Leuconostoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leuconostoc "Leuconostoc")* variety is found more dominantly in kimchi fermentation because of its lower acid tolerance and microaerophilic properties; the *[Leuconostoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leuconostoc "Leuconostoc")* variety also grows better at low salt concentrations.[[82]](#cite_note-:9-83) Throughout the fermentation process, as acidity rises, the *[Lactobacillus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus "Lactobacillus")* and *[Weissella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weissella "Weissella")* variety become dominant because of their higher acid tolerance. *Lactobacillus* also grows better in conditions with a higher salt concentration.[[82]](#cite_note-:9-83)

These microorganisms are present due to the natural microflora provided by utilizing unsterilized food materials in the production of kimchi.[[85]](#cite_note-:0-86)[[86]](#cite_note-:1-87) The step of salting the raw materials as well as the addition of red pepper powder inhibits the pathogenic and putrefactive bacteria present in the microflora, allowing the [lactic acid bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactic_acid_bacteria "Lactic acid bacteria") (LAB) to flourish and become the dominant microorganism.[[85]](#cite_note-:0-86)[[87]](#cite_note-88) These anaerobic microorganisms steadily increase in number during the middle stages of fermentation, and prefer to be kept at low temperatures of about 10 °C, pH of 4.2-4, and remain in the presence of 1.5% – 4% NaCl.[[82]](#cite_note-:9-83)[[85]](#cite_note-:0-86) A faster fermentation at a higher temperature may be chosen as well to accelerate the growth of bacterial cultures for a faster decrease in pH level.[[82]](#cite_note-:9-83)

Since the raw cruciferous vegetables themselves are the source of LAB required for fermentation, no starter culture is required for the production of kimchi; rather, spontaneous fermentation occurs.[[88]](#cite_note-:2-89) The total population of microorganisms present at the beginning of processing determines the outcome of fermentation, causing the final product to be highly variable in terms of quality and flavor.[[85]](#cite_note-:0-86) Currently, there are no recommended approaches to control the microbial community during fermentation to predict the outcome.[[88]](#cite_note-:2-89) In the industrial production of kimchi, starter cultures made up of *[Leu. mesenteroides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leuconostoc_mesenteroides "Leuconostoc mesenteroides")*, *[Leu. citreum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leuconostoc_citreum "Leuconostoc citreum")*, and *[Lb. plantarum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactiplantibacillus_plantarum "Lactiplantibacillus plantarum")* are used, which are often unsuccessful because they fail to outcompete the naturally occurring cultures on the raw materials.[[82]](#cite_note-:9-83)

### By-products of microorganisms

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: By-products of microorganisms")]

The [lactic acid bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactic_acid_bacteria "Lactic acid bacteria") (LAB) produce [lactic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactic_acid "Lactic acid"), [hydrogen peroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_peroxide "Hydrogen peroxide"), and [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide "Carbon dioxide") as by-products during metabolism. Lactic acid quickly lowers the pH, creating an acidic environment that is uninhabitable for most other microorganisms that survived salting.[[86]](#cite_note-:1-87) This also modifies the flavor of sub-ingredients and can increase the nutritive value of the raw materials, as the microbial community in the fermentation process can synthesize B vitamins and hydrolyze cellulose in plant tissues to free nutrients that are normally indigestible by the human gastrointestinal tract.[[86]](#cite_note-:1-87) Hydrogen peroxide is formed by the oxidation of reduced [nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicotinamide_adenine_dinucleotide "Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide") (NADH) and provides an antibiotic to inhibit some undesirable microorganisms.[[86]](#cite_note-:1-87) Carbon dioxide functions as a preservative, flushing out oxygen to create an anaerobic environment, as well as creating the desired carbonation in the final product.[[86]](#cite_note-:1-87)

### Odor

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Odor")]

Kimchi is known for its strong, spicy, flavors and odors, although milder varieties exist. Variations in the fermentation process cause the final product to be highly variable in terms of quality and flavor.[[85]](#cite_note-:0-86) The strong odor is especially tied to the sulfur compounds from garlic and ginger of kimchi, which can be less appealing to non-Koreans. Thus, scientists are experimenting with the types of bacteria used in its production to minimize the odor to increase the appeal to international markets.[[89]](#cite_note-Fifield_6/22/2017-90) These efforts are not universally appreciated by lovers of kimchi, as the flavor is affected in the process, and some see that "South Korea's narrative about its own culinary staple" is being manipulated to suit the foreigners' tastes.[[90]](#cite_note-Herreria_Russo_6/29/2017-91)

Varieties
---------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Varieties")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/09/Korean.cuisine-Banchan-02.jpg/250px-Korean.cuisine-Banchan-02.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Korean.cuisine-Banchan-02.jpg)

*[Tongkimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongkimchi?action=edit&redlink=1 "Tongkimchi (page does not exist)")*, *gulgimchi* (kimchi with additional oyster) and other [banchan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banchan "Banchan")

Kimchi is one of the most important staples of [Korean cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_cuisine "Korean cuisine"). The Korean term "Kimchi" refers to fermented vegetables, and encompasses salt and seasoned vegetables.[[91]](#cite_note-Jang_et_al_2016-92) It is mainly served as a side dish with every meal, but also can be served as a main dish.[[92]](#cite_note-93) Kimchi is mainly recognized as a spicy fermented cabbage dish globally.[[23]](#cite_note-btpickle-201309-24)

New variations of kimchi continue to be created,[[93]](#cite_note-kimchi-types-94) and the taste can vary depending on the region and season.[[94]](#cite_note-95) Conventionally, the secret of kimchi preparation was passed down by mothers to their daughters in a bid to make them suitable wives to their husbands.[[95]](#cite_note-Chung_et_al_2017-96) However, with the current technological advancement and increase in social media use, many individuals worldwide can now access recipes for kimchi preparation.[[95]](#cite_note-Chung_et_al_2017-96)

Kimchi can be categorized by main ingredients, regions or seasons. Korea's northern and southern sections have a considerable temperature difference.[[96]](#cite_note-yahookorea-97) There are over 180 recognized varieties of kimchi.[[97]](#cite_note-Korea_Tourism_Organization-98) The most common kimchi variations are:

* *[Baechu-kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baechu-kimchi "Baechu-kimchi")* (배추김치) spicy napa cabbage kimchi, made from whole cabbage leaves
* *Baechu-geotjeori* (배추겉절이) unfermented napa cabbage kimchi
* *Bossam-kimchi* (보쌈김치) wrapped kimchi
* *[Baek-kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baek-kimchi "Baek-kimchi")* (백김치) white kimchi, made without chili pepper
* *[Dongchimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dongchimi "Dongchimi")* (동치미) a non-spicy watery kimchi
* *[Nabak-kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nabak-kimchi "Nabak-kimchi")* (나박김치) a mildly spicy watery kimchi
* *Chonggak-kimchi* (총각김치) cubed [chonggak "ponytail" radish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chonggak_radish "Chonggak radish"), a popular spicy kimchi
* *[Kkakdugi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kkakdugi "Kkakdugi")* (깍두기) spicy cubed [Korean radish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_radish "Korean radish") strongly-scented kimchi containing fermented shrimp
* *Oi-sobagi* (오이소박이) cucumber kimchi that can be stuffed with seafood and chili paste, and is a popular choice during the spring and summer seasons
* *[Pa-kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pa-kimchi "Pa-kimchi")* (파김치) spicy green onion kimchi
* *[Yeolmu-kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeolmu-kimchi "Yeolmu-kimchi")* (열무김치) is also a popular choice during the spring and summer, and is made with [yeolmu radishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeolmu_radish "Yeolmu radish"), and does not necessarily have to be fermented.
* *[Gat-kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gat-kimchi "Gat-kimchi")* (갓김치), made with [Indian mustard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_mustard "Indian mustard")
* *Yangbaechu-kimchi* (양배추 김치) spicy cabbage kimchi, made from "headed" [cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabbage "Cabbage") leaves (as opposed to napa cabbage)

Kimchi from the northern parts of Korea tend to have less salt and red chili and usually do not include brined seafood for seasoning. Northern kimchi often has a watery consistency. Kimchi made in the southern parts of Korea, such as [Jeolla Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeolla_Province "Jeolla Province") and [Gyeongsang Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gyeongsang_Province "Gyeongsang Province"), uses salt, chili peppers and *[myeolchijeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myeolchijeot "Myeolchijeot")* (멸치젓, brined [anchovy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchovies_as_food "Anchovies as food") allowed to ferment) or *[saeujeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saeujeot "Saeujeot")* (새우젓, brined shrimp allowed to ferment), *myeolchiaekjeot* (멸치액젓), anchovy fish sauce, *kkanariaekjeot* (까나리액젓), liquid anchovy [jeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeotgal "Jeotgal"), similar to [fish sauce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fish_sauce "Fish sauce") used in Southeast Asia, but thicker.

*[Saeujeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saeujeot "Saeujeot")* (새우젓) or *myeolchijeot* is not added to the kimchi spice-seasoning mixture, but is simmered first to reduce odors, eliminate tannic flavor and fats, and then is mixed with a thickener made of rice or wheat starch (풀). This technique has been falling into disuse in the past 40 years.

### Color

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Color")]

[White kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_kimchi "White kimchi") is neither red nor spicy. It includes white [napa cabbage kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baechu-kimchi "Baechu-kimchi") and other varieties such as white [radish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_radish "Korean radish") kimchi (*[dongchimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dongchimi "Dongchimi")*). Watery white kimchi varieties are sometimes used as an ingredient in a number of dishes such as cold noodles in *dongchimi* [brine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brine "Brine") (*dongchimi-guksu*).

### Seasonal variations

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section: Seasonal variations")]

Different types of kimchi were traditionally made at different times of the year, based on when various vegetables were in season and also to take advantage of hot and cold seasons before the era of refrigeration. Although the advent of modern refrigeration – including [kimchi refrigerators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimchi_refrigerator "Kimchi refrigerator") specifically designed with precise controls to keep different varieties of kimchi at optimal temperatures at various stages of fermentation – has made this seasonality unnecessary, Koreans continue to consume kimchi according to traditional seasonal preferences.[[100]](#cite_note-101)

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/53/Korean-Dongchimi-01.jpg/250px-Korean-Dongchimi-01.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Korean-Dongchimi-01.jpg)

*[Dongchimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dongchimi "Dongchimi") (동치미)* is largely served during winter. Dongchimi is also used to make buckwheat *[naengmyeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naengmyeon "Naengmyeon")*, a popular dish during hot months.

#### Spring

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=28 "Edit section: Spring")]

After a long period of consuming *[gimjang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimjang "Gimjang") kimchi* (김장김치) during the winter, fresh [potherbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potherb "Potherb") and vegetables were used to make kimchi. These kinds of kimchi were not fermented or even stored for long periods of time but were consumed fresh.

#### Summer

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=29 "Edit section: Summer")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/Yeolmukimchi_3.jpg/250px-Yeolmukimchi_3.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Yeolmukimchi_3.jpg)

[*Yeolmu*-kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeolmu-kimchi "Yeolmu-kimchi"), cold, watery kimchi that is usually eaten with oily foods, is consumed mostly in the summer.

[Yeolmu radishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeolmu_radish "Yeolmu radish") and cucumbers are summer vegetables made into kimchi, *[yeolmu-kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeolmu-kimchi "Yeolmu-kimchi")* (열무김치) which is eaten in several bites. Brined fish or shellfish can be added, and freshly ground dried chili peppers are often used.

#### Autumn

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=30 "Edit section: Autumn")]

*[Baechu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baechu "Baechu") kimchi* is prepared by inserting blended stuffing materials, called *sok* (literally *inside*), between layers of salted leaves of uncut, whole [Napa cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napa_cabbage "Napa cabbage"). The ingredients of *sok* (속) can vary, depending on the regions and weather conditions. Generally, *baechu kimchi* used to have a strong salty flavor until the late 1960s, before which a large amount of *[myeolchijeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myeolchijeot "Myeolchijeot")* or *[saeujeot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saeujeot "Saeujeot")* had been used.

*Gogumasoon Kimchi* is made from sweet potato stems.

#### Winter

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=31 "Edit section: Winter")]

Traditionally, the greatest varieties of kimchi were available during the winter. In preparation for the long winter months, many types of [kimjang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimjang "Gimjang") kimchi (김장 김치) were prepared in early winter and stored in the ground in large kimchi pots. Today, many city residents use modern [kimchi refrigerators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimchi_refrigerator "Kimchi refrigerator") offering precise temperature controls to store kimjang kimchi. November and December are traditionally when people begin to make kimchi; women often gather together in each other's homes to help with winter kimchi preparations.[[47]](#cite_note-nyt2010-48) "Baechu kimchi" is made with salted [baechu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baechu "Baechu") filled with thin strips of [radish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_radish "Korean radish"), parsley, pine nuts, pears, chestnuts, shredded red pepper, [manna lichen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbilicaria_esculenta "Umbilicaria esculenta") ([Korean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_language "Korean language"): 석이 버섯; [RR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revised_Romanization_of_Korean "Revised Romanization of Korean"): *seogi beoseot*), garlic, and ginger.

### Korean preference

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=32 "Edit section: Korean preference")]

As of 2004, the preference of kimchi preparation in Korean households from the most prepared type of kimchi to less prepared types of kimchi was: *baechu* kimchi, being the most prepared type of kimchi, then *[kkakdugi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kkakdugi "Kkakdugi")*, then *[dongchimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dongchimi "Dongchimi")* and then *chonggak* kimchi. *Baechu* kimchi comprised more than seventy percent of marketed kimchi and radish kimchi comprised about twenty percent of marketed kimchi.[[101]](#cite_note-Hui2004-102)

Nutrition
---------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=33 "Edit section: Nutrition")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/70/Korean_stew-Kimchi_jjigae-05.jpg/250px-Korean_stew-Kimchi_jjigae-05.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Korean_stew-Kimchi_jjigae-05.jpg)

*[Kimchi jjigae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimchi_jjigae "Kimchi jjigae")* (김치찌개), a stew made of kimchi, vegetables, broth, and other ingredients, is a popular dish during the cold months.

Kimchi is made of various vegetables and contains a high concentration of [dietary fiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietary_fiber "Dietary fiber"),[[102]](#cite_note-103) while being low in [food energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_energy "Food energy"). The vegetables used in kimchi also contribute to intake of vitamin A, thiamine (B1), riboflavin (B2), calcium, and iron.[[103]](#cite_note-Asianinfo-104)[[104]](#cite_note-tour2korea-105)

Trade
-----

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=34 "Edit section: Trade")]

South Korea spent around $129 million in 2017 to purchase 275,000 metric tons of foreign kimchi, more than 11 times the amount it exported, according to data released by the Korea Customs Service in 2017.[[108]](#cite_note-auto-109) South Korea consumes 1.85 million metric tons of kimchi annually, or 36.1 kg per person.[[109]](#cite_note-110) It imports a significant fraction of that, mostly from China, and runs a $47.3 million kimchi trade deficit.[[108]](#cite_note-auto-109)

Consumption
-----------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=35 "Edit section: Consumption")]

In 2021, Koreans collectively consumed 1,965,000 tons of Kimchi, with average Korean consuming 88.3 grams of Kimchi daily. This average has been steadily declining from 109.9 grams per day in 2010, marking a 19.6% decrease. Males tend to consume more Kimchi than females, with an average of 106.6 grams compared to 70.0 grams.[[111]](#cite_note-112)

Food regulations
----------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Kimchi&action=edit&section=36 "Edit section: Food regulations")]

The [Canadian Food Inspection Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Food_Inspection_Agency "Canadian Food Inspection Agency") has regulations for the commercial production of kimchi. The final product should have a pH ranging from 4.2 to 4.5.[[112]](#cite_note-:4-113) Any low-acidity ingredients with a pH above 4.6, including white daikon and napa cabbage, should not be left under conditions that enable the growth of undesirable microorganisms and require a written illustration of the procedure designed to ensure this is available if requested.[[112]](#cite_note-:4-113) This procedural design should include steps that maintain sterility of the equipment and products used, and the details of all sterilization processes.[[112]](#cite_note-:4-113) The cutoff pH of 4.6 is a value common to many food safety regulations, initially defined because [botulism toxin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botulism_toxin "Botulism toxin") is not produced below this level.[[113]](#cite_note-114)
