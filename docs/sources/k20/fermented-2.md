Pasteurized vs. Unpasteurized Sauerkraut: How to Tell the Difference (2026)
===========================================================================

Not all sauerkraut is the same. Pasteurization kills the live probiotics that make fermented foods beneficial. Whether you’re shopping or checking what’s already in your fridge, you’ll never be confused again.

Why It Matters: The Difference Between Pasteurized and Unpasteurized Sauerkraut
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Standing in the grocery aisle staring at two jars of sauerkraut that look almost identical, you might wonder if the choice really matters. It does, and the distinction is simple: pasteurization uses heat to kill microorganisms, including the beneficial bacteria that make fermented foods valuable for gut health. Unpasteurized sauerkraut retains those live cultures, offering probiotics and enzymes that pasteurization destroys.
This guide will teach you exactly how to tell the difference between pasteurized and unpasteurized sauerkraut using five reliable methods, what to do when labels are confusing, and how to make informed choices for your health. Whether you shop at supermarkets, farmers markets, or buy online, you will leave here knowing exactly what is inside that jar.

Biological Background: What Happens During Fermentation and Pasteurization
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

### The Natural Process of Lacto-Fermentation

Sauerkraut is made through lacto-fermentation, a natural process where lactic acid bacteria (LAB) convert sugars in cabbage into lactic acid. This acid acts as a natural preservative, creating the tangy flavor and sour aroma that defines sauerkraut. The key players are bacteria from the *Lactobacillus* genus, along with *Leuconostoc* and *Pediococcus* species.
During fermentation, these bacteria multiply rapidly, creating a complex ecosystem of microorganisms. The resulting brine becomes cloudy, tiny bubbles appear as carbon dioxide is released, and the pH drops to around 3.5 to 4.0. This acidic environment prevents harmful pathogens from growing while preserving the cabbage for months.

### What Pasteurization Actually Does

Pasteurization involves heating the sauerkraut to temperatures between 60°C and 85°C (140°F to 185°F) for a specific period. This heat treatment kills microorganisms, including the beneficial lactic acid bacteria. The goal is to extend shelf life and create a consistent, shelf-stable product that does not require refrigeration.
The trade-off is significant. Live cultures, enzymes, and heat-sensitive vitamins like vitamin C are substantially reduced or eliminated. The sauerkraut remains edible and retains fiber and some minerals, but its probiotic value is lost.

### Legal Definitions and Regulatory Standards

In the United States, the FDA defines pasteurization as any heat treatment that reduces pathogenic microorganisms to a level that does not pose a public health risk. For sauerkraut, there is no single federal standard that mandates pasteurization, but most commercially canned sauerkraut undergoes heat treatment to ensure shelf stability.
The term "unpasteurized" is not strictly regulated for sauerkraut, which means manufacturers may use it inconsistently. However, when a product is labeled "raw" or "contains live cultures," it must be unpasteurized by definition. Understanding these nuances helps you read beyond marketing language.

Health Relevance: Why the Difference Matters for Your Body
----------------------------------------------------------

### Probiotic Benefits of Live Cultures

Unpasteurized sauerkraut contains live lactic acid bacteria that can survive digestion and reach the colon. Research published in the journal *Food Microbiology* has shown that these bacteria can transiently colonize the gut, supporting digestive health and immune function. The specific strains found in sauerkraut, such as *Lactobacillus plantarum* and *Lactobacillus brevis*, have been studied for their ability to reduce inflammation and improve gut barrier function.
Pasteurized sauerkraut, by contrast, offers no probiotic benefit. The heat treatment kills these bacteria, leaving behind only fiber, sodium, and some nutrients. If you are eating sauerkraut specifically for gut health, unpasteurized is the only option that delivers live cultures.

### Enzyme Activity and Nutrient Availability

Fermentation naturally produces enzymes that aid digestion. Myrosinase, an enzyme found in cabbage, is activated during fermentation and helps break down glucosinolates into compounds with potential anti-inflammatory properties. Heat treatment inactivates these enzymes, reducing the potential digestive benefits.
Vitamin C is also heat-sensitive. Unpasteurized sauerkraut can contain 15 to 30 milligrams of vitamin C per 100 grams, while pasteurized versions may have significantly less due to heat degradation. The fermentation process itself can also increase the bioavailability of certain minerals, though pasteurization does not completely eliminate this effect.

### Individual Variability in Response

Not everyone responds to probiotics the same way. Some people experience bloating or gas when first introducing live fermented foods, while others notice improved digestion immediately. Your gut microbiome composition, diet, and overall health status influence how your body handles live cultures.
Pasteurized sauerkraut may be more appropriate for individuals with compromised immune systems, as the live bacteria in unpasteurized products could theoretically pose a risk. However, for most healthy adults, unpasteurized sauerkraut is safe and beneficial.

Method 1: Where You Find It in the Store (The Quickest Clue)
------------------------------------------------------------

### Refrigerated Section vs. Shelf-Stable Aisle

The single most reliable indicator of unpasteurized sauerkraut is its location in the store. Unpasteurized sauerkraut requires refrigeration to slow down fermentation and prevent spoilage. You will find it in the refrigerated section, typically near pickles, kimchi, and other fermented vegetables.
Shelf-stable sauerkraut, found in the canned goods aisle or on dry shelves, is almost always pasteurized. The heat treatment allows it to sit at room temperature without spoiling for months or even years.

### Important Exceptions to Watch For

Some refrigerated sauerkraut products have been pasteurized. Manufacturers sometimes pasteurize then refrigerate the product to extend shelf life while maintaining a "fresh" appearance. This is more common in deli cases where sauerkraut is sold in bulk or from large containers.
Conversely, some shelf-stable products labeled "naturally fermented" may contain live cultures if they are packaged in a way that allows fermentation to continue. However, this is rare. Always verify with additional methods.

### Farmer's Markets and Specialty Stores

Sauerkraut sold at farmer's markets or from small batch producers is typically unpasteurized. These producers rely on refrigeration to maintain quality and often highlight the live culture content. If you are buying from a local vendor, ask directly whether the product is pasteurized.

Method 2: Read the Label Like a Pro
-----------------------------------

### Positive Indicators for Unpasteurized Sauerkraut

Look for these terms on the label:

* **Raw** – Legally means no heat treatment, so live cultures remain
* **Unpasteurized** – Direct statement that the product has not been heat treated
* **Contains live cultures** – Indicates viable bacteria are present
* **Live & active cultures** – Similar to the above, common on refrigerated products
* **Naturally fermented** – Suggests traditional fermentation, but verify with other clues
* **No heat treatment** – Direct statement of unpasteurized status
* **Keep refrigerated** – Strong indicator of live cultures needing cold storage

### Red Flags for Pasteurized Sauerkraut

Terms that suggest pasteurization include:

* **Pasteurized** – Obvious, but sometimes in small print
* **Heat treated** – Same as pasteurized
* **Ultra-pasteurized** – Higher heat treatment, no live cultures
* **Shelf-stable** – Implies heat treatment or preservatives
* **No refrigeration needed** – Clear indicator of pasteurization
* **Unopened shelf life 2+ years** – Only possible with heat treatment or preservatives

### Common Brand Examples

Bubbies sauerkraut is unpasteurized and clearly labeled "contains live cultures." Wildbrine also offers unpasteurized options with "live & active cultures" on the label. On the other hand, Frank's sauerkraut in cans is pasteurized, and many store brands in cans are as well. Some brands like Cleveland Kraut offer both refrigerated (unpasteurized) and shelf-stable (pasteurized) versions, so reading the label is essential.

Method 3: Check the Ingredients - What's In and What's Out
----------------------------------------------------------

### The Simple Formula for Unpasteurized Sauerkraut

Authentic unpasteurized sauerkraut contains only:

* **Cabbage** – The primary ingredient
* **Salt** – Typically 2-3% by weight, needed for fermentation
* **Water** – Sometimes added, sometimes just the natural cabbage juice

That is it. Some traditional recipes include caraway seeds, juniper berries, or other spices, but the core formula remains simple. The fermentation process itself creates the acidity and flavor, so no additional acids are needed.

### Ingredients That Signal Pasteurization

When you see any of the following, the sauerkraut is likely pasteurized or at least not traditionally fermented:

* **Vinegar** – Added to provide artificial sourness without fermentation. This is a major red flag
* **Sugar** – Sometimes added to balance acidity in pasteurized products
* **Preservatives** – Sodium benzoate, potassium sorbate, or sodium metabisulfite are used to extend shelf life
* **Natural flavors** – A vague term that can mask shortcuts in fermentation
* **Citric acid** – Added acidity, not from fermentation
* **Lactic acid** – Sometimes added to mimic the taste of fermentation without the process
* **Wine or other acids** – Unnecessary in traditional sauerkraut

### Why Vinegar Is a Red Flag

Vinegar is added to pasteurized sauerkraut to create a sour taste quickly without waiting for fermentation to occur. Real fermented sauerkraut produces lactic acid naturally, which has a different flavor profile than acetic acid from vinegar. If you see vinegar in the ingredients list, the product is not traditionally fermented and likely contains no live cultures.

Method 4: Visual and Sensory Tests (Bubbles, Brine, and Smell)
--------------------------------------------------------------

### Cloudy Brine vs. Clear Brine

Unpasteurized sauerkraut typically has a cloudy brine. This cloudiness comes from the lactic acid bacteria that are suspended in the liquid, along with other fermentation byproducts. The cloudiness may settle over time but can be redistributed by gently shaking the jar.
Pasteurized sauerkraut has a clear or nearly clear brine. The heat treatment kills the bacteria, and the liquid becomes translucent. If you see a jar with perfectly clear liquid, it is almost certainly pasteurized.

### Bubbles: Signs of Active Fermentation

Tiny bubbles rising from the sauerkraut or clinging to the sides of the jar indicate ongoing fermentation. These bubbles are carbon dioxide produced by the lactic acid bacteria. Even in refrigerated unpasteurized sauerkraut, slow fermentation continues, producing small bubbles.
Pasteurized sauerkraut shows no bubbles. The liquid is still and the kraut looks inactive. If you see bubbles, you are looking at live cultures at work.

### Smell and Taste Differences

Unpasteurized sauerkraut has a complex, tangy, and slightly sour aroma with a noticeable depth. The flavor is bright and acidic but not harsh. The longer it ferments, the more pungent it becomes.
Pasteurized sauerkraut smells milder and less complex. The flavor is often one-dimensional sourness, sometimes with a vinegary sharpness. Some people describe it as "flat" compared to raw sauerkraut.

### Texture: Crunch vs. Soft

Unpasteurized sauerkraut retains more crunch because the cabbage structure has not been broken down by heat. The fermentation process softens the cabbage slightly, but it remains firm and crisp.
Pasteurized sauerkraut is often softer, sometimes mushy, because the heat treatment breaks down cell walls. This is especially noticeable in canned sauerkraut, which has a cooked texture.

### Important Caveat

Some pasteurized sauerkraut may still show slight fizzing or bubbling if it was not fully heated or if it has been contaminated with wild yeasts after opening. Visual and sensory tests are helpful but not definitive on their own.

Method 5: Check the Packaging (Canned vs. Jarred vs. Pouch)
-----------------------------------------------------------

### Metal Cans: Always Pasteurized

Sauerkraut sold in metal cans is always pasteurized. The canning process requires high heat to create a vacuum seal and ensure shelf stability. There is no way to produce live-culture sauerkraut in a sealed metal can because the heat treatment would kill the bacteria.
If you see sauerkraut in a can, it is pasteurized and contains no probiotics.

### Glass Jars: Can Be Either

Glass jars are used for both pasteurized and unpasteurized sauerkraut. The packaging itself does not tell you which type you have. Some glass jars are shelf-stable (pasteurized), while others are refrigerated (unpasteurized).
Look for the "keep refrigerated" label on glass jars. If it is not there, the product is likely pasteurized.

### Pouches and Bags: Usually Refrigerated

Sauerkraut sold in plastic pouches or bags is typically refrigerated and unpasteurized. These packages are designed to keep the product cold and allow gas exchange from fermentation. Some brands use vacuum-sealed pouches that still contain live cultures.

### Bulk and Deli Containers

Sauerkraut sold in bulk from deli cases or large plastic tubs may be either pasteurized or unpasteurized. Ask the store staff or check for labels indicating live cultures. Many deli sauerkrauts are pasteurized for safety and consistency.

What If the Label Is Ambiguous? A Decision Tree
-----------------------------------------------

### Step-by-Step Logic for Confusing Products

When you encounter a product with unclear labeling, use this decision tree:

1. **Is it refrigerated?** Yes → Likely unpasteurized. No → Likely pasteurized.
2. **Does it say "contains live cultures" or "raw"?** Yes → Unpasteurized. No → Proceed to next step.
3. **Does it say "naturally fermented" but is shelf-stable?** This is ambiguous. Check ingredients.
4. **Does it contain vinegar, sugar, or preservatives?** Yes → Pasteurized or not traditionally fermented. No → Proceed to next step.
5. **Is the brine cloudy?** Yes → Likely unpasteurized. No → Likely pasteurized.
6. **Are there bubbles?** Yes → Unpasteurized. No → Inconclusive.
7. **Still unsure?** Contact the manufacturer using the information on the label.

### Understanding "Naturally Fermented" on Shelf-Stable Products

Some manufacturers use the term "naturally fermented" on shelf-stable jars. This usually means the cabbage was fermented traditionally, but the final product was then pasteurized for shelf stability. The term refers to the fermentation process, not the final state of the product.
If you see "naturally fermented" on a shelf-stable jar, the product likely started as fermented sauerkraut but was then heated to kill the cultures. It will not contain live probiotics.

### Contacting the Manufacturer

When in doubt, contact the manufacturer. Most companies have customer service email addresses or phone numbers on their website. Ask specifically: "Is your sauerkraut pasteurized after fermentation? Does it contain live lactic acid bacteria at the time of purchase?"
Reputable companies will give you a direct answer. If they are evasive or vague, that is a red flag.

### pH Testing as a Definitive Method

For those who want scientific certainty, pH testing can help. Unpasteurized sauerkraut typically has a pH between 3.5 and 4.0. Pasteurized sauerkraut may have a similar pH if vinegar or lactic acid was added, but the pH alone does not confirm live cultures.
However, if the pH is above 4.5, the product is likely not properly fermented and may be pasteurized. pH strips are inexpensive and available at most grocery or pharmacy stores.

Special Scenario: Homemade vs. Store-Bought
-------------------------------------------

### Homemade Sauerkraut

Homemade sauerkraut is always unpasteurized unless you deliberately heat treat it. The fermentation process relies on natural lactic acid bacteria, and no heat is applied. Homemade kraut is packed with live cultures and enzymes.
If you are making sauerkraut at home, you have full control over the process. Simply ferment cabbage with salt, wait 1 to 4 weeks depending on temperature and preference, and then refrigerate to slow fermentation.

### Farmer's Market Finds

Sauerkraut from farmer's markets is usually unpasteurized, but always ask. Small producers often take pride in their live culture products and will be happy to explain their process. Look for signs of refrigeration and ask about heat treatment.

### Online and Mail Order Sauerkraut

When ordering sauerkraut online, check the shipping method. Unpasteurized sauerkraut must be shipped with cold packs and expedited delivery to maintain freshness. If the product arrives at room temperature, it may have been pasteurized or may have lost its live cultures during transit.

What If You Already Bought Pasteurized? Can You Make It Alive Again?
--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Why You Cannot Re-Introduce Live Cultures

Once sauerkraut has been pasteurized, the live bacteria are dead and cannot be revived. You cannot add probiotics to pasteurized sauerkraut and expect them to grow because the environment is not conducive to bacterial growth. The acidity, salt content, and lack of fermentable sugars mean that simply adding a probiotic supplement will not work.

### Practical Uses for Pasteurized Sauerkraut

Pasteurized sauerkraut is still edible and can be used for cooking. Because the live cultures are dead, you can heat it without worrying about killing probiotics. Use it for:

* **Reuben sandwiches** – The heat from cooking would kill probiotics anyway
* **Soup and stews** – Adds flavor and fiber
* **Braised dishes** – Pairs well with pork, sausages, and potatoes
* **Baked dishes** – Casseroles and bakes

### How to Choose Better Next Time

Use the five methods in this guide to select unpasteurized sauerkraut on your next shopping trip. Keep a mental checklist: refrigerated, live cultures label, simple ingredients, cloudy brine, and glass jar or pouch.

What Microbiome Testing May Reveal
----------------------------------

### Understanding Your Gut Ecosystem

Your gut microbiome is the collection of trillions of microorganisms living in your digestive tract. These bacteria, fungi, and other microbes play a crucial role in digestion, immune function, mood regulation, and metabolism. The balance of different species varies from person to person, influenced by diet, genetics, medications, and lifestyle.

### How Live Fermented Foods Affect the Microbiome

Consuming unpasteurized sauerkraut introduces live lactic acid bacteria into your gut. While these bacteria typically do not permanently colonize the gut, they can transiently influence the microbial community. Research has shown that regular consumption of fermented foods can increase microbial diversity and reduce inflammatory markers.
Pasteurized sauerkraut, lacking live cultures, does not provide this microbial benefit. It still contributes fiber and nutrients, but the direct impact on the microbiome is minimal.

### Individual Variability in Response

Some people notice significant improvements in digestion after adding unpasteurized sauerkraut to their diet, while others see little change. This variability depends on your existing gut microbiome composition, the presence of specific bacterial species, and your overall dietary patterns.

### Limitations of Guessing Your Gut Health

It is difficult to determine how your gut microbiome responds to specific foods based on symptoms alone. Bloating, gas, or changes in bowel habits can result from many factors, including food intolerances, stress, or medication use. A food diary and symptom tracking can help, but they cannot reveal the underlying microbial composition.

### Who May Benefit from Understanding Their Microbiome

Individuals with persistent digestive issues, unexplained food sensitivities, or a history of antibiotic use may benefit from a deeper understanding of their gut microbiome. Microbiome testing can provide insights into the diversity and composition of your gut bacteria, helping you make more informed dietary choices.

### Educational Value of Microbiome Testing

Testing your microbiome is an educational tool that can reveal:

* **Microbial diversity** – Higher diversity is generally associated with better health
* **Specific bacterial populations** – Levels of beneficial vs. potentially harmful bacteria
* **Functional potential** – What your microbes are capable of doing
* **Comparison to healthy populations** – How your microbiome compares to reference groups

This information can guide dietary choices, including whether live fermented foods like unpasteurized sauerkraut might be particularly beneficial for your unique gut ecosystem.

Frequently Asked Questions About Pasteurized vs. Unpasteurized Sauerkraut
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

### How can you tell if store-bought sauerkraut is pasteurized?

Check the store location first. If it is on a shelf at room temperature, it is pasteurized. If refrigerated, check the label for "contains live cultures" or "raw." Look at the ingredients for vinegar or preservatives, which signal pasteurization. The brine should be cloudy and possibly show bubbles if unpasteurized.

### Is all sauerkraut in the refrigerated section unpasteurized?

No, not always. Some refrigerated sauerkraut has been pasteurized and then refrigerated for freshness. Always check the label for terms like "pasteurized," "heat treated," or "contains live cultures." When in doubt, contact the manufacturer.

### What does "naturally fermented" on a shelf-stable jar mean?

This usually means the cabbage was fermented traditionally but then pasteurized for shelf stability. The term refers to the fermentation process, not the final product. Shelf-stable sauerkraut labeled "naturally fermented" typically does not contain live probiotics.

### Can pasteurized sauerkraut have any probiotics?

No. Pasteurization kills all microorganisms, including beneficial bacteria. Pasteurized sauerkraut contains no live probiotics. The only way to get probiotics from sauerkraut is to choose unpasteurized, refrigerated products with live cultures.

### How long does unpasteurized sauerkraut last in the fridge?

Unpasteurized sauerkraut can last 4 to 6 months in the refrigerator if kept sealed and submerged in brine. Once opened, it should be consumed within 2 to 4 weeks for best quality. Always check for signs of spoilage such as mold, off odors, or slimy texture.

### What are the best unpasteurized sauerkraut brands?

Bubbies, Wildbrine, Cleveland Kraut (refrigerated line), and Farmhouse Culture are widely available unpasteurized brands. Small batch producers at farmers markets also offer excellent options. Always verify the label for "contains live cultures" or "raw."

### Is raw sauerkraut safe to eat?

Yes, for most healthy adults. The lactic acid produced during fermentation creates an acidic environment that prevents harmful pathogens from growing. People with compromised immune systems, pregnant women, or those with certain medical conditions should consult a healthcare provider before consuming raw fermented foods.

### Can you cook with unpasteurized sauerkraut without killing probiotics?

Cooking above 45°C (113°F) will kill the live bacteria. To preserve probiotics, add unpasteurized sauerkraut to dishes after cooking, or use it as a cold topping. If you plan to cook the sauerkraut, you can use pasteurized versions since the probiotics would be lost anyway.

### Does pasteurized sauerkraut taste different?

Yes. Unpasteurized sauerkraut has a complex, tangy flavor with depth and brightness. Pasteurized sauerkraut tastes milder, often one-dimensionally sour, and may have a vinegary note if vinegar was added. Many people find unpasteurized sauerkraut more flavorful.

### Why do some sauerkraut jars say "keep refrigerated" but others don't?

Jars that say "keep refrigerated" contain live cultures that need cold temperatures to slow fermentation and prevent spoilage. Jars without this instruction are pasteurized and shelf-stable. The "keep refrigerated" label is one of the most reliable indicators of unpasteurized sauerkraut.

Practical Interpretation: Making Informed Choices
-------------------------------------------------

Understanding the difference between pasteurized and unpasteurized sauerkraut empowers you to make choices aligned with your health goals. If you want probiotics and enzymes, choose unpasteurized sauerkraut from the refrigerated section with a simple ingredient list and cloudy brine. If you are cooking or prefer a milder flavor, pasteurized options work fine.
Remember that the best choice depends on your individual needs. Some people thrive on live fermented foods, while others may need to introduce them gradually. Listening to your body and paying attention to how you feel after eating different types of sauerkraut can guide your decisions.

Conclusion
----------

The difference between pasteurized and unpasteurized sauerkraut is straightforward once you know what to look for. By using the five methods outlined in this guide — store location, label reading, ingredient check, visual and sensory tests, and packaging analysis — you can confidently determine whether any sauerkraut product contains live cultures.
The most reliable indicators are refrigeration, "contains live cultures" on the label, a simple ingredient list with only cabbage and salt, cloudy brine, and visible bubbles. Canned sauerkraut is always pasteurized, while glass jars require closer inspection.
