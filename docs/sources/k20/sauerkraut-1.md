From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Finely sliced and fermented cabbage

**Sauerkraut** ([/ˈsaʊ.ərˌkraʊt/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); German: [[ˈzaʊ.ɐˌkʁaʊt]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Standard_German "Help:IPA/Standard German") [ⓘ](/wiki/File:De-sauerkraut.ogg "File:De-sauerkraut.ogg"), lit. 'sour cabbage')[[1]](#cite_note-etym-1) is finely cut raw white [cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabbage "Cabbage") that has been [fermented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactic_acid_fermentation "Lactic acid fermentation") by various [lactic acid bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactic_acid_bacteria "Lactic acid bacteria").[[2]](#cite_note-farnworth1-2)[[3]](#cite_note-fao1-3) It has a long [shelf life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelf_life "Shelf life") and a distinctive [sour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sourness "Sourness") flavor, both of which result from the [lactic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactic_acid "Lactic acid") formed when the bacteria ferment the carbohydrates in the cabbage leaves.[[4]](#cite_note-GilMarks-4)

History
-------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sauerkraut&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b5/Kiszona_kapusta.JPG/250px-Kiszona_kapusta.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Kiszona_kapusta.JPG)

Polish *kapusta kiszona*

[Fermented foods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermented_foods "Fermented foods") have a long history in many cultures. The [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome "Ancient Rome") writers [Cato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_the_Elder "Cato the Elder") (in his *[De agri cultura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_agri_cultura "De agri cultura")*) and [Columella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Columella "Lucius Columella") (in his *[De re Rustica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Columella#De_re_Rustica "Lucius Columella")*) mentioned preserving cabbages and [turnips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnips "Turnips") with salt. According to Wilhelm Holzapfel et al., Plinius the Elder, writing in the first century AD, is reputed to have been the first writer to describe the making of sauerkraut by preserving what the Romans called *salt cabbage* in earthen vessels.[[5]](#cite_note-Holzapfel2003-5) Popular folklore has imagined that sauerkraut was introduced to Europe by the [trade networks formed across Eurasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Mongolica#Trade_network "Pax Mongolica") by the [Golden Horde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Horde "Golden Horde"). However, according to Mack and Surina (2005), there is no evidence to support this theory, nor any evidence that fermented cabbage arrived from an East Asian source, and there is evidence of sauerkraut production in Europe dating back to the early period of the [Western Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Roman_Empire "Western Roman Empire").[[6]](#cite_note-Mack_Surina-6)

Before [frozen foods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frozen_food "Frozen food"), refrigeration, and cheap transport from warmer areas became readily available in [Northern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Europe "Northern Europe"), [Central](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Europe "Central Europe"), and [Eastern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Europe "Eastern Europe"), sauerkraut – like other preserved foods – provided a source of certain nutrients (eg, [vitamin C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_C "Vitamin C")) during the winter. Captain [James Cook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cook "James Cook") took sauerkraut on his sea voyages to prevent [scurvy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scurvy "Scurvy").[[7]](#cite_note-O'Sullivan2008-7)[[8]](#cite_note-Makepeace2018-8)

Name
----

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sauerkraut&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Name")]

The English name is borrowed from German, where it means "sour cabbage".[[1]](#cite_note-etym-1) The dish did not originate in Germany. Some claim fermented cabbage, *[Suan cai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suan_cai "Suan cai")*, was already produced in the days of the building of the [Great Wall of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China "Great Wall of China").[[9]](#cite_note-9) However, the Romans pickled forms of cabbage, and were the more likely source of modern-day European sauerkraut.[[10]](#cite_note-10) It became popular in Central and Eastern European cuisines, and countries including the Netherlands, where it is known as *zuurkool*, and France, where the name became *choucroute*.[[11]](#cite_note-11) According to Mack and Surina (2005), the Slavic peoples of Europe likely discovered fermented cabbage on their own.[[6]](#cite_note-Mack_Surina-6)

The names in Slavic and other [Central and Eastern European](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_and_Eastern_Europe "Central and Eastern Europe") languages have similar meanings with the German word: "fermented cabbage" ([Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_language "Armenian language"): թթու կաղամբ, [Albanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanian_language "Albanian language"): *lakër turshi*, [Azerbaijani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azerbaijani_language "Azerbaijani language"): *kələm turşusu*,[[12]](#cite_note-12)[Belarusian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_language "Belarusian language"): квашаная капуста, [Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_language "Czech language"): *kysané zelí*, [Lithuanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_language "Lithuanian language"): *rauginti kopūstai*, [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language "Polish language"): *kapusta kiszona/ kapusta kwaszona*, [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language "Russian language"): квашеная капуста/кислая капуста, [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Russian "Romanization of Russian"): *kvašenaja kapusta*, [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_language "Turkish language"): *lahana turşusu*, [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_language "Romanian language"): *varză murată*, [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language "Persian language"): *kalam torş*, [Bulgarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian_language "Bulgarian language"): кисело зеле, [Estonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_language "Estonian language"): *hapukapsas*, [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language "Hungarian language"): *savanyúkáposzta*, [Latvian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvian_language "Latvian language"): *skābēti kāposti*, [Macedonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonian_language "Macedonian language"): расол / кисела зелка, [Serbo-Croatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbo-Croatian_language "Serbo-Croatian *kvashena kapusta*).[[13]](#cite_note-EEF-13)

The word *[Kraut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kraut "Kraut")*, derived from this food, is a derogatory term for the German people.[[14]](#cite_note-14) During [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I "World War I"), due to concerns the American public would reject a product with a German name, American sauerkraut makers [relabeled their product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_politically_motivated_renamings "List of politically motivated renamings") as "liberty cabbage" for the duration of the war.[[15]](#cite_note-[[New_York_Times]]_Article-15)

Production
----------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sauerkraut&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Production")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/9b/Saurkraut_%28cropped%29.JPG/250px-Saurkraut_%28cropped%29.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Saurkraut_(cropped).JPG)

Homemade sauerkraut

Sauerkraut is made by a process of [pickling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pickling "Pickling") called [lactic acid fermentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactic_acid_fermentation "Lactic acid fermentation"), analogous to how traditional (not heat-treated) [pickled cucumbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pickled_cucumber "Pickled cucumber") and [kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimchi "Kimchi") are made. The cabbage is finely shredded, layered with salt, and left to ferment. Fully cured sauerkraut keeps for several months in an airtight container stored at 15 °C (60 °F) or below. In temperate climates, this allows storage over the full winter and early-spring. Neither refrigeration nor [pasteurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasteurization "Pasteurization") is required, although these treatments prolong storage life.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Fermentation by [lactobacilli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus "Lactobacillus") is introduced naturally, as these air-borne bacteria culture on raw cabbage leaves where they grow. Yeasts also are present, and may yield soft sauerkraut of poor flavor when the fermentation temperature is too high. The fermentation process has three phases, collectively sometimes referred to as population dynamics. In the first phase, [anaerobic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaerobic_organism "Anaerobic organism") bacteria such as *[Klebsiella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klebsiella "Klebsiella")* and *[Enterobacter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enterobacter "Enterobacter")* lead the fermentation, and begin producing an acidic environment that favors later bacteria. The second phase starts as the acid levels become too high for many bacteria, and *[Leuconostoc mesenteroides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leuconostoc_mesenteroides "Leuconostoc mesenteroides")* and other *Leuconostoc* species take dominance. In the third phase, various *Lactobacillus* species, including *[L. brevis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus_brevis "Lactobacillus brevis")* and *[L. plantarum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus_plantarum "Lactobacillus plantarum")*, ferment any remaining sugars, further lowering the [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH "PH").[[a]](#cite_note-17) Properly cured sauerkraut is sufficiently [acidic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acidic "Acidic") to prevent a favorable environment for the growth of *[Clostridium botulinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clostridium_botulinum "Clostridium botulinum")*, the toxins of which cause [botulism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botulism "Botulism").[[2]](#cite_note-farnworth1-2)[[3]](#cite_note-fao1-3)

A 2004 [genomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genomics "Genomics") study found an unexpectedly large diversity of lactic acid bacteria in sauerkraut, and that previous studies had oversimplified this diversity. *[Weissella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weissella "Weissella")* was found to be a major organism in the initial, heterofermentative stage, up to day 7. It was also found that *[Lactobacillus brevis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus_brevis "Lactobacillus brevis")* and *[Pediococcus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pediococcus "Pediococcus") pentosaceus* had smaller population numbers in the first 14 days than previous studies had reported.[[17]](#cite_note-18)

The Dutch sauerkraut industry found that combining a new batch of sauerkraut with an old batch resulted in an exceedingly sour product. This [sourdough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sourdough "Sourdough") process is known as "backslopping" or "inoculum enrichment"; when used in making sauerkraut, first- and second-stage population dynamics, important to developing flavor, are bypassed. This is due primarily to the greater initial activity of species *L. plantarum*.[[18]](#cite_note-19)

Regional varieties
------------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sauerkraut&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Regional varieties")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f6/Cavolo_salato.jpg/250px-Cavolo_salato.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cavolo_salato.jpg)

Eastern European-style sauerkraut pickled with carrots and served as a salad

In [Armenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenian_cuisine "Armenian cuisine"), [Belarusian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belarusian_cuisine "Belarusian cuisine"), [Estonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_cuisine "Estonian cuisine"), [Latvian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvian_cuisine "Latvian cuisine"), [Lithuanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuanian_cuisine "Lithuanian cuisine"), [Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_cuisine "Czech cuisine"), [Slovak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovak_cuisine "Slovak cuisine"), [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_cuisine "Polish cuisine"), [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_cuisine "Russian cuisine"), and [Ukrainian cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_cuisine "Ukrainian cuisine"), chopped cabbage is often pickled together with shredded [carrots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrot "Carrot"). Other ingredients may include [caraway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caraway "Caraway") seeds, whole or quartered [apples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple "Apple") for additional flavor or [cranberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranberry "Cranberry") for flavor and better keeping (the [benzoic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzoic_acid "Benzoic acid") in cranberries is a common preservative). [Bell peppers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_pepper "Bell pepper") and [beets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beets "Beets") are added in some recipes for color. The resulting sauerkraut salad is typically served cold, as *[zakuski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zakuski "Zakuski")* or a side dish.[[19]](#cite_note-20)[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

A homemade type of very mild sauerkraut is available, where white cabbage is pickled with salt in a refrigerator for only three to seven days. This process results in very little lactic acid production. Sometimes in Russia double fermentation is used, with the initial step producing an exceptionally sour product, which is then "corrected" by adding 30–50% more fresh cabbage and fermenting the mix again. The flavor additives like apples, beets, cranberries, and sometimes even [watermelons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon "Watermelon") are usually introduced at this step.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Sauerkraut may be used as a filling for [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_cuisine "Polish cuisine") *[pierogi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierogi "Pierogi")*, Ukrainian *[varenyky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varenyky "Varenyky")*, Russian *[pirogi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirog "Pirog")* and *[pirozhki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pirozhki "Pirozhki")*.[[20]](#cite_note-21) Sauerkraut is also the central ingredient in traditional soups, such as *[shchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shchi "Shchi")* (a [national dish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_dish "National dish") of Russia), *[kwaśnica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabbage_soup "Cabbage soup")* (Poland), *[kapustnica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabbage_soup "Cabbage soup")* ([Slovakia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slovak_cuisine "Slovak cuisine")), [*kapusniak*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabbage_soup "Cabbage soup") ([Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukrainian_cuisine "Ukrainian cuisine")) and *[zelňačka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabbage_soup "Cabbage soup")* ([Czech Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_cuisine "Czech cuisine") resp. Moravian). It is an ingredient of Polish *[bigos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bigos "Bigos")* (a hunter's stew).[[21]](#cite_note-22)

In [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_cuisine "German cuisine") and [Austria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_cuisine "Austrian cuisine"), cooked sauerkraut is often flavored with [juniper berries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juniper_berry "Juniper berry")[[22]](#cite_note-23) or [caraway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caraway "Caraway") seeds; apples and white wine are added in popular variations. In [South Tyrol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Tyrol "South Tyrol"), it is made with juniper berries, [extra-virgin olive oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extra-virgin_olive_oil "Extra-virgin olive oil") and smoked [pancetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pancetta "Pancetta"). Traditionally it is served warm, with pork (such as *[eisbein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eisbein "Eisbein")*, *[schweinshaxe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweinshaxe "Schweinshaxe")*, *[Kassler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kassler "Kassler")*) or [sausages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sausage "Sausage") (smoked or fried sausages, *[Frankfurter Würstchen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankfurter_W%C3%BCrstchen "Frankfurter Würstchen")*, [Vienna sausages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna_sausage "Vienna sausage"), [black pudding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_pudding "Black pudding")), accompanied typically by roasted or steamed potatoes or dumplings (*[knödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kn%C3%B6del "Knödel")* or *[schupfnudel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schupfnudel "Schupfnudel")*).[[23]](#cite_note-24) Similar recipes are common in other Central European cuisines. The [Czech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_cuisine "Czech cuisine") [national dish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_dish "National dish") *vepřo knedlo zelo* consists of roast pork with *[knedlíky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kn%C3%B6del "Knödel")* and sauerkraut.

In Bulgaria, Montenegro, Serbia, Bosnia, Croatia, North Macedonia and Slovenia, usually the [whole cabbage heads are pickled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whole_sour_cabbage "Whole sour cabbage"). They can be used for many dishes, from a simple salad made of shredded cabbage seasoned with paprika or black pepper, to cabbage rolls such as [sarma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarma_(food) "Sarma (food)") or [arambaši](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramba%C5%A1i "Arambaši"). In Bulgarian cuisine sauerkraut is known as кисело зеле, and many traditional Bulgarian dishes are made with sauerkraut, like свинско със зеле (pork with sauerkraut), сарми (cabbage rolls), зелник (Bulgarian banitsa with sauerkraut), капама (sausage and meat with cabbage rolls and sauerkraut in clay pot), боб със зеле (beans with sauerkraut) and салата от кисело зеле с червен пипер (sauerkraut salad with paprika). In northern parts of Serbia and Croatia, it is often added to bean soup. In central Serbia, a local specialty called "[wedding cabbage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedding_cabbage "Wedding cabbage")" is made by slowly stewing roughly cut cabbage with at least three kinds of meats, lean, fatty, and smoked.

In Szeklerland it is an essential part of *székelygulyás* and the *erdélyi rakott káposzta* (a type of casserole).

In Romania, the local type of sauerkraut (*varza murata*, whole pickled cabbage heads)[[24]](#cite_note-25) are used as wrap for the national dish, *[sarmale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarmale "Sarmale")*, a Turkish-inspired roll, made of pickled cabbage leaves with minced pork and rice, having its own personality and very distinct in taste from its Ottoman predecessor.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In France, sauerkraut is the main ingredient of the [Alsatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alsatian_cuisine "Alsatian cuisine") meal *[choucroute garnie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choucroute_garnie "Choucroute garnie")* ([French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language "French language") for 'dressed sauerkraut'), sauerkraut with sausages (Strasbourg sausages, smoked [Morteau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morteau_Sausage "Morteau Sausage") or [Montbéliard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montb%C3%A9liard "Montbéliard") sausages), [charcuterie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charcuterie "Charcuterie") (bacon, ham, etc.), and often [potatoes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potato "Potato").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

In Chile it is called *chucrut* and is a common topping for sandwiches and hotdogs, especially for [completos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completo "Completo").[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

Sauerkraut, along with [pork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pork "Pork"), is eaten traditionally in [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania "Pennsylvania") on New Year's Day. The tradition, started by the [Pennsylvania Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania_Dutch "Pennsylvania Dutch"), is thought to bring good luck for the upcoming year.[[25]](#cite_note-26) Sauerkraut is also used in [American cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisine_of_the_United_States "Cuisine of the United States") as a [condiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condiment "Condiment") upon various foods, such as sandwiches and hot dogs.[[4]](#cite_note-GilMarks-4)[[26]](#cite_note-27) In [Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maryland "Maryland"), particularly in [Baltimore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore "Baltimore") and on the Eastern Shore, sauerkraut is a traditional accompaniment for the Thanksgiving turkey.[[27]](#cite_note-28)

As Europeans, especially Germans, emigrated to other countries, many of them continued making and eating sauerkraut around the world.[[28]](#cite_note-29) It has also been popular among [Ashkenazi Jews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jews "Ashkenazi Jews").[[29]](#cite_note-30)

Many health benefits have been claimed for sauerkraut:

* It is a high source of vitamins [K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_K "Vitamin K") and (if uncooked) [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitamin_C "Vitamin C");[[31]](#cite_note-nutritiondb-32) the fermentation process increases the [bioavailability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bioavailability "Bioavailability") of nutrients rendering sauerkraut even more nutritious than the original cabbage.[[32]](#cite_note-lipski1-33) It is also low in [food energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_energy "Food energy") and high in [calcium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calcium "Calcium") and [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium "Magnesium"), and it is a very good source of [dietary fiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietary_fiber "Dietary fiber"), [folate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folate "Folate"), [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron "Iron"), [potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium "Potassium"), [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper "Copper") and [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese "Manganese").[[31]](#cite_note-nutritiondb-32)
* If [unpasteurized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unpasteurized "Unpasteurized") and uncooked, sauerkraut also contains live [lactobacilli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lactobacillus "Lactobacillus") and beneficial microbes and is rich in [enzymes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme "Enzyme"). Fiber and [probiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probiotics "Probiotics") improve digestion and promote the growth of healthy bowel flora, protecting against many diseases of the digestive tract.[[32]](#cite_note-lipski1-33)[[33]](#cite_note-healthbank1-34)
* During the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War "American Civil War"), the physician [John Jay Terrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Jay_Terrell?action=edit&redlink=1 "John Jay Terrell (page does not exist)") (1829–1922)[[34]](#cite_note-35) was able to successfully reduce the death rate from disease among prisoners of war; he attributed this to feeding his patients raw sauerkraut.[[35]](#cite_note-36)
* Sauerkraut and its juice is a time-honored [folk remedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_remedy "Folk remedy") for [canker sores](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphthous_stomatitis "Aphthous stomatitis"). The treatment is to rinse the mouth with sauerkraut juice for about 30 seconds several times a day, or place a wad of sauerkraut against the affected area for a minute or so before chewing and swallowing the sauerkraut.[[36]](#cite_note-latimes1-37)
* In 2002, the *Journal of Agriculture and Food Chemistry* reported that Finnish researchers found the [isothiocyanates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isothiocyanates "Isothiocyanates") produced in sauerkraut fermentation inhibit the growth of [cancer cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer_cell "Cancer cell") in test tube and animal studies.[[37]](#cite_note-38) A Polish study in 2010 concluded that "[induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enzyme_induction_and_inhibition "Enzyme induction and inhibition") of the key detoxifying enzymes by cabbage juices, particularly sauerkraut, may be responsible for their chemopreventive activity demonstrated by epidemiological studies and in animal models".[[38]](#cite_note-39)[[39]](#cite_note-40)[[40]](#cite_note-rdve-41)[[41]](#cite_note-hfdt-42)[[42]](#cite_note-denn-43)[[43]](#cite_note-eenc-44)[[44]](#cite_note-abdec-45)[[45]](#cite_note-ecka-46)
* Sauerkraut was one of the foods taken by English explorer James Cook on his first and second pacific voyages as a way to prevent scurvy.[[46]](#cite_note-47)
* [Romani people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people "Romani people") consider it a lucky food.[[47]](#cite_note-48)

### Disadvantages

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sauerkraut&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Disadvantages")]

Excessive consumption of sauerkraut may lead to [bloating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloating "Bloating") and [flatulence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatulence "Flatulence") due to the [trisaccharide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trisaccharide "Trisaccharide") [raffinose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raffinose "Raffinose"), which the human [small intestine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_intestine "Small intestine") cannot break down. This does not negatively affect long-term health, although it might be uncomfortable.[[48]](#cite_note-St._John2011-49) Additionally, sauerkraut has a very high [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt "Salt") content.[[49]](#cite_note-50)

Similar foods
-------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Sauerkraut&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Similar foods")]

Many other vegetables are preserved by a similar fermentation pickling process:

* *[Achaar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaar "Achaar")* in India, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Pakistan

* [Dill pickles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dill_pickles "Dill pickles") in eastern and central Europe
* *[Encurtido](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encurtido "Encurtido")* in Central America
* *[Kimchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimchi "Kimchi")* in Korea
* [Silage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silage "Silage"), a feed for cattle
* *[Suan cai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suan_cai "Suan cai")* in northeastern China
* *[Tsukemono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsukemono "Tsukemono")* in Japan
