A **Danish pastry** (sometimes shortened to **Danish**; [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language "Danish language"): *wienerbrød*[[ˈviˀnɐˌpʁœðˀ]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Danish "Help:IPA/Danish"), lit. '[Viennese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna "Vienna") bread') is a multilayered, [laminated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laminated_dough "Laminated dough") sweet [viennoiserie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viennoiserie "Viennoiserie"). Like other viennoiserie, such as [croissants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croissant "Croissant"), it is neither a bread nor a [pastry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastry "Pastry"), as it uses [yeast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baker's_yeast "Baker's yeast")-leavened [dough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dough "Dough"), that is laminated like [puff pastry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puff_pastry "Puff pastry") to create a layered texture.

It is thought that some [bakery techniques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakery "Bakery") were brought to [Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark "Denmark") by [Austrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrians "Austrians") [bakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakery "Bakery"), and originated the name of this product. The Danish recipe is however different from the Viennese one and has since developed into a Danish specialty. The origin of the product itself is also not clear.[[1]](#cite_note-Davidson-1)

Danish pastries were brought with immigrants to the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States "United States"), where they are often topped with a fruit or cream cheese filling, and are now popular around the world.[[2]](#cite_note-cphpost.dk-2)

Terminology
-----------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Danish_pastry&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Terminology")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Spandauer_med_creme.JPG/250px-Spandauer_med_creme.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Spandauer_med_creme.JPG)

A common version of the pastry in Denmark, Norway, Finland, and Sweden[[3]](#cite_note-3)[[4]](#cite_note-4)[[5]](#cite_note-5)[[6]](#cite_note-6)

In [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language "Danish language"), [Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_language "Norwegian language"), and [Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_language "Swedish language"), the term for Danish pastry is *wienerbrød* (or *wienerbröd*), meaning 'Viennese bread'.[[7]](#cite_note-7)[[8]](#cite_note-:0-8) The same etymology is also the origin of the [Icelandic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_language "Icelandic language") *vínarbrauð*, [Faroese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faroese_language "Faroese language") *vinarbreyð*, [Finnish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finnish_language "Finnish language") *viineri* and [Estonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_language "Estonian language") *Viini sai* ('Viennese pastry').

In Austria and Bavaria, conversely, the Danish pastry is known as *Kopenhagener Plunder* (or simply *Kopenhagener*, after [Copenhagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen "Copenhagen")) or *Dänischer Plunder*.[[1]](#cite_note-Davidson-1)[[9]](#cite_note-9)[[10]](#cite_note-10)[[8]](#cite_note-:0-8)

Composition
-----------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Danish_pastry&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Composition")]

Danish pastry is made of [yeast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baker's_yeast "Baker's yeast")-leavened [dough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dough "Dough") (known as a viennoiserie) of wheat [flour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flour "Flour"), [milk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milk "Milk"), [eggs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egg_(food) "Egg (food)"), [sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sugar "Sugar"), and large amounts of [butter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butter "Butter") or [margarine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margarine "Margarine").[[11]](#cite_note-11)

A yeast dough is rolled out thinly, covered with thin slices of butter between the layers of dough, and then the dough is folded and rolled several times, creating 27 layers.[[12]](#cite_note-12)[[13]](#cite_note-Pie_and_Pastry_Bible-13) If necessary, the dough is chilled between foldings to ease handling. The process of rolling, buttering, folding, and chilling is repeated multiple times to create a multilayered dough that becomes airy and crispy on the outside, but also rich and buttery.[[13]](#cite_note-Pie_and_Pastry_Bible-13)

Butter is the traditional fat used in Danish pastry,[[14]](#cite_note-14) but in industrial production, less expensive fats are often used, such as [hydrogenated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fat_hydrogenation "Fat hydrogenation") [sunflower oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower_oil "Sunflower oil").

History
-------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Danish_pastry&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: History")]

The origin of the Danish pastry is often ascribed to a strike amongst bakery workers in Denmark in 1850. The strike caused bakery owners to hire workers from abroad, among them several Austrian bakers, who brought along new baking traditions and recipes. The Austrian pastry of *[Plundergebäck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plundergeb%C3%A4ck?action=edit&redlink=1 "Plundergebäck (page does not exist)") [[de](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plunderteig "de:Plunderteig")]* soon became popular in Denmark and after the labour disputes ended, Danish bakers adopted the Austrian recipes, adjusting them to their own liking and traditions by increasing the amount of egg and fat for example. This development resulted in what is now known as the Danish pastry.[[15]](#cite_note-15)[[16]](#cite_note-16)

One of the baking techniques and traditions that the Austrian bakers reportedly brought with them was the Viennese [lamination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laminated_dough "Laminated dough") technique.[[17]](#cite_note-17) Due to such novelties the Danes called the pastry *wienerbrød* and that name is still in use in Northern Europe today.[[18]](#cite_note-18) At that time, almost all baked goods in Denmark were given [exotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exoticism "Exoticism") names.[[2]](#cite_note-cphpost.dk-2)

As consumed in Denmark
----------------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Danish_pastry&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: As consumed in Denmark")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Cinnamon_danish_in_Copenhagen.jpg/250px-Cinnamon_danish_in_Copenhagen.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Cinnamon_danish_in_Copenhagen.jpg)

A cinnamon Danish with chocolate and nuts from a bakery in Denmark

Danish pastries as consumed in Denmark have different shapes, sizes, and names. Some are topped with chocolate, [pearl sugar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_sugar "Pearl sugar"), [glacé icing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icing_(food) "Icing (food)"), and/or slivered nuts and they may be stuffed with a variety of ingredients such as jam or preserves (usually apple or prune), [remonce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remonce "Remonce"), [marzipan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marzipan "Marzipan"), and/or custard. Shapes are numerous, including circles with filling in the middle (known in Denmark as *Spandauers*), figure-eights, spirals (known as snails), and huge pretzel-like [kringles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kringle "Kringle").[[19]](#cite_note-19)[[20]](#cite_note-20) There is also the *frøsnapper*, which is a twisted pastry sprinkled with sesame and poppy seeds. The pastry is often filled with marzipan or custard.[[21]](#cite_note-21)

Danish pastries of all variations have come to symbolize *[hygge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hygge "Hygge")*, a significant concept of Danish culture that embodies a sense of coziness and comfort.

As consumed elsewhere
---------------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Danish_pastry&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: As consumed elsewhere")]

In Finland, Sweden, and Norway, Danish pastry is typically made in the *Spandauer*-style, often with vanilla custard.

In the UK, various ingredients such as [jam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jam "Jam"), [custard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custard "Custard"), [apricots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apricot "Apricot"), [cherries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cherry "Cherry"), [raisins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raisin "Raisin"), flaked [almonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almond "Almond"), [pecans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pecan "Pecan"), or [caramelized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caramelized "Caramelized") [toffee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toffee "Toffee") are placed on or within sections of divided dough, which is then baked. [Cardamom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardamom "Cardamom") is often added to enhance the aroma.

In the US, Danishes are typically given a topping of fruit and/or sweetened cream cheese prior to baking. Danishes with nuts on them are also popular there and in Sweden, where often icing, and, sometimes, powdered sugar and chocolate spritzing are also added.

In Argentina, they are usually filled with [dulce de leche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulce_de_leche "Dulce de leche") or [dulce de membrillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dulce_de_membrillo "Dulce de membrillo").

As consumed in United States
----------------------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Danish_pastry&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: As consumed in United States")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e0/Danish_%281%29.jpg/250px-Danish_%281%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Danish_(1).jpg)

A slice of an American apple crumb Danish

Danish pastry was brought to the United States by [Danish immigrants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_Americans "Danish Americans"). Lauritz C. Klitteng of [Læsø](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A6s%C3%B8 "Læsø") popularized "Danish pastry" in the US around 1915–1920. According to Klitteng, he made Danish pastry for the wedding of [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_United_States "President of the United States") [Woodrow Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson "Woodrow Wilson") in December 1915. Klitteng toured the world to promote his product and was featured in such 1920s periodicals as the *National Baker*, the *Bakers' Helper*, and the *Bakers' Weekly*. Klitteng briefly had his own Danish Culinary Studio at 146 Fifth Avenue in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City "New York City").[[22]](#cite_note-22)

Herman Gertner owned a chain of New York City restaurants and had brought Klitteng to New York to sell Danish pastry. Gertner's obituary appeared in the January 23, 1962 *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")*:[[23]](#cite_note-23)

> At one point during his career Mr. Gertner befriended a Danish baker who convinced him that Danish pastry might be well received in New York. Mr. Gertner began serving the pastry in his restaurant and it immediately was a success.
