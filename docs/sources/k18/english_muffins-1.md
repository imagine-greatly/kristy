An **English muffin** is a small, round, flat [yeast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeast "Yeast")-leavened (sometimes [sourdough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sourdough "Sourdough")) bread which is commonly about 4 in (10 cm) in diameter and 1.5 in (4 cm) tall. It is generally split horizontally and served [toasted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toast_(food) "Toast (food)").[[2]](#cite_note-david-2) They are unsweetened and frequently eaten with sweet or savoury toppings, such as butter, fruit jam, honey, eggs, sausage, bacon, or cheese. English muffins are an essential ingredient in [eggs Benedict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eggs_Benedict "Eggs Benedict") and a variety of [breakfast sandwiches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakfast_sandwich "Breakfast sandwich") derived from it, such as the [McMuffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McMuffin "McMuffin").

Rather than being entirely oven-baked, they are also cooked in a [griddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griddle "Griddle") on the stove top and flipped from side-to-side, which results in a flattened shape rather than the rounded top seen in baked rolls or cake-type muffins.[[3]](#cite_note-3)

Of English origin, they are called *English muffins* to distinguish them from the sweeter cupcake-shaped [quick breads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quick_bread "Quick bread") originating from the United States which are also known as [muffins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muffins "Muffins"), although in the UK, English muffins are sometimes referred to simply as *muffins*[[4]](#cite_note-Myers_2018-4) or *breakfast muffins*.[[5]](#cite_note-warburtons-5) English muffins are available in a wide range of varieties, including [whole wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whole_wheat "Whole wheat"), [multigrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multigrain_bread "Multigrain bread"), cinnamon raisin, [cranberry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranberry "Cranberry"), and apple cinnamon.

Origin
------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=English_muffin&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Origin")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/Paul_Sandby_-_London_Cries-_A_Muffin_Man_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg/250px-Paul_Sandby_-_London_Cries-_A_Muffin_Man_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Paul_Sandby_-_London_Cries-_A_Muffin_Man_-_Google_Art_Project.jpg)

*London Cries: A Muffin Man* (c. 1759)

The word *muffin* is thought to come from the [Low German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_German "Low German") *muffen*, meaning "little cakes".[[6]](#cite_note-6) The *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary "Oxford English Dictionary")* also suggests a possible link to [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French "Old French") *moflet*, a type of bread. Originally it meant "any of various kinds of bread or cake".[[7]](#cite_note-7)

The first recorded use of the word *muffin* was in 1703,[[8]](#cite_note-8) and recipes for muffins appear in British cookery books as early as 1747 in [Hannah Glasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hannah_Glasse "Hannah Glasse")'s *[The Art of Cookery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_Cookery_Made_Plain_and_Easy "The Art of Cookery Made Plain and Easy")*, although the product is undoubtedly older than that.[[9]](#cite_note-9) The muffins are described by Glasse as being "like a Honey-comb" inside.[[10]](#cite_note-10)

In the *Oxford Companion to Food*, [Alan Davidson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Davidson_(food_writer) "Alan Davidson (food writer)") states that "[t]here has always been some confusion between muffins, [crumpets, and pikelets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crumpet "Crumpet"), both in recipes and in name".[[11]](#cite_note-Davidson-11) The English muffin has been described as a variant form of a crumpet, or a "cousin", with the difference being the location of the holes; in a crumpet, the holes go all the way to the top, whereas with an English muffin, the holes are inside.[[12]](#cite_note-Nibble1-12)

### Muffin men

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=English_muffin&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Muffin men")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/36/Muffin_man_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_20338.png/250px-Muffin_man_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_20338.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Muffin_man_-_Project_Gutenberg_eText_20338.png)

A Victorian-era muffin man ringing a bell, *[Punch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punch_(magazine) "Punch (magazine)")*, 1892

The increasing popularity of flatbread muffins by the mid-18th century is attested by the existence of muffin men, a type of [hawker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawker_(trade) "Hawker (trade)") who would travel door to door selling English muffins as a snack bread before most homes had their own ovens.[[11]](#cite_note-Davidson-11)

The bell-ringing of muffin men became so common that by 1839 the British Parliament passed a bill[[13]](#cite_note-13) to prohibit it, but this was not adhered to by sellers.[[14]](#cite_note-14) In 1861, "goodsized" muffins from street-sellers were commonly sold for a [halfpenny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halfpenny_(British_pre-decimal_coin) "Halfpenny (British pre-decimal coin)") each; crumpets were about a penny.[[15]](#cite_note-15)

Comparing the bell-ringing of muffin men to the melodic chimes from an approaching [ice cream van](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_cream_van "Ice cream van") that generates excitement in children today, Michael Paterson writes in *A Brief History of Life in Victorian Britain*, "the ringing of a handbell was one of the most joyous sounds in a Victorian childhood".[[16]](#cite_note-Paterson-16) The tradition of the muffin man continued until the Second World War.[[16]](#cite_note-Paterson-16)

### United Kingdom

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=English_muffin&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: United Kingdom")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6c/Tesco_muffins.jpg/250px-Tesco_muffins.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tesco_muffins.jpg)

A wholemeal English muffin, whole, split, toasted and topped with butter

Both English muffins and sweet American-style cupcake-shaped muffins are referred to as *muffins* in the UK, although the terms *English muffin*, *breakfast muffin*, or *toasting muffin* are often used to indicate the former, and legislation refers to the latter as *American muffins*.[[17]](#cite_note-17)[[18]](#cite_note-18)[[4]](#cite_note-Myers_2018-4)[[19]](#cite_note-19) They are usually consumed with tea or coffee, and sometimes feature in [afternoon tea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afternoon_tea "Afternoon tea") served in UK hotels.[[20]](#cite_note-20)[[21]](#cite_note-21)[[22]](#cite_note-22) They are also consumed for [breakfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakfast "Breakfast") in the form of American-style breakfast sandwiches.[[23]](#cite_note-23)[[24]](#cite_note-24)[[25]](#cite_note-25)

### United States

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=English_muffin&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: United States")]

"Mush muffins (called *slipperdowns* in New England) were a Colonial [American] muffin made with [hominy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hominy "Hominy") on a hanging griddle."[[26]](#cite_note-26) These and other types of flatbread muffins were known to American settlers, but they declined in popularity with the advent of the quickbread muffin.

References to English muffins appear in U.S. newspapers starting in 1859,[[27]](#cite_note-27)[[28]](#cite_note-28)[[29]](#cite_note-29) and detailed descriptions of them and recipes were published as early as 1870.[[30]](#cite_note-30)[[31]](#cite_note-31)

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f1/Muffin_House_watering_jeh.jpg/250px-Muffin_House_watering_jeh.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Muffin_House_watering_jeh.jpg)

The "Muffin House" in Manhattan, home of Samuel Bath Thomas' second bakery, and ancestor of [Thomas' English Muffins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas' "Thomas'")

A popular brand of English muffin in the U.S. is [Thomas'](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas' "Thomas'"), which was founded in Manhattan, New York, by English immigrant Samuel Bath Thomas in 1880.[[32]](#cite_note-32) He reintroduced the flatbread muffin to the American market. Thomas called the product "toaster crumpets", and intended them as a "more elegant alternative to toast' to be served in fine hotels.[[12]](#cite_note-Nibble1-12) Thomas opened a second bakery around the corner from the first at 337 West 20th Street in a building that remains known as "The Muffin House".[[33]](#cite_note-33) Today the company is owned by [Bimbo Bakeries USA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimbo_Bakeries_USA "Bimbo Bakeries USA"), which also owns the [Entenmann's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entenmann's "Entenmann's"), Boboli, Stroehmann, Oroweat, and Arnold brands.[[34]](#cite_note-34)

In 1910, Fred Wolferman of Kansas City, Missouri began making denser English muffins at his family grocery, using empty tin cans as molds.[[35]](#cite_note-35)

[Foster's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bickford's_(restaurant)#Foster's_English_muffins "Bickford's (restaurant)") sourdough English muffins was a popular brand of English muffin originally from [San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco "San Francisco"). They were a signature menu item at Foster's restaurants from the 1940s to the 1970s, and continued to be produced as a packaged brand until 2008.[*[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")*]

### Bakeware

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=English_muffin&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Bakeware")]

[Muffin rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muffin_rings "Muffin rings") are metal cookware used for oven-baking or griddle-cooking flatbread muffins. They are circle-shaped objects made of thin metal. The rings are about one inch high.

A "muffineer" was originally a sugar shaker, looking like a large salt cellar with a perforated decorative top, for spreading powdered sugar on muffins and other sweet cakes. Later, in the 19th century, the term was also used to describe a silver, or silver-plated, muffin dish, with a domed lid and a compartment below for hot water, used to keep toasted English muffins warm before serving.

The traditional English nursery rhyme "[The Muffin Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Muffin_Man "The Muffin Man")", which dates from 1820 at the latest, traces to that custom.[[37]](#cite_note-|Douce_Adds_134(8)_no._3-37)

A well-known reference to English muffins is in [Oscar Wilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Wilde "Oscar Wilde")'s 1895 play *[The Importance of Being Earnest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Importance_of_Being_Earnest "The Importance of Being Earnest")*.[[38]](#cite_note-38)
