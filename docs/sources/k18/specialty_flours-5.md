**Einkorn**  (from German *Einkorn*, literally "single grain") can refer to either a wild species of [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat "Wheat") (*Triticum*) or a [domesticated form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_wheat "Common wheat") of wheat. The wild form is ***T. boeoticum*** (syn. *T. m.* subsp. *boeoticum*), and the domesticated form is ***T. monococcum*** (syn. *T. m.* subsp. *monococcum*). Einkorn is a [diploid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diploid "Diploid") species (2*n* = 14 chromosomes) of hulled wheat, with tough [glumes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glume "Glume") ([husks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Husk "Husk")) that tightly enclose the [grains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain "Grain"). The cultivated form is similar to the wild, except that the [ear stays intact when ripe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shattering_(agriculture) "Shattering (agriculture)")[[1]](#cite_note-Complex-Origins-1) and the seeds are larger. The domestic form is known as *petit épeautre* in French, *Einkorn* in German, "einkorn" or "littlespelt" in English, *piccolo farro* in Italian and *escanda menor* in Spanish.[[2]](#cite_note-LeBrun1992-2) The name refers to the fact that each [spikelet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spikelet "Spikelet") contains only one grain.[[3]](#cite_note-3) Einkorn was one of the first forms of wheat to be [cultivated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crop_farming "Crop farming").

Description
-----------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Einkorn&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Description")]

Einkorn is a short variety of wild wheat, usually less than 70 centimetres (28 in) tall, and is not very productive of edible seeds.[[4]](#cite_note-ZoharyHopf3343-4) The principal difference between wild einkorn and cultivated einkorn is the method of seed dispersal. In the wild variety, the seed head usually shatters and drops the kernels (seeds) of wheat onto the ground.[[1]](#cite_note-Complex-Origins-1) This facilitates a new crop of wheat. In the domestic variety, the seed head remains intact. While such a [mutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation "Mutation") may occasionally occur in the wild, it is not viable there in the long term: the intact seed head will only drop to the ground when the stalk rots, and the kernels will not scatter but form a tight clump which inhibits germination and makes the mutant seedlings susceptible to disease. Harvesting einkorn with intact seed heads was easier for early human harvesters, who could then manually break apart the seed heads and scatter any kernels not eaten. Over time and through selection, conscious or unconscious, the human preference for intact seed heads created the domestic variety, which has slightly larger kernels than wild einkorn. Domesticated einkorn thus requires human planting and harvesting for its continuing existence.[[5]](#cite_note-5) This process of domestication may have taken only 20 to 200 years, resulting in a wheat that was easier to harvest.[[6]](#cite_note-6)

An important characteristic facilitating the domestication of einkorn and other annual grains is that the plants are largely self-pollinating. Thus, the desirable (for human management) traits of einkorn could be perpetuated at less risk of cross-fertilization with wild plants which might have traits – e.g. smaller seeds, shattering seed heads,[[1]](#cite_note-Complex-Origins-1) as less desirable for human management.[[7]](#cite_note-7)

Taxonomy
--------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Einkorn&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Taxonomy")]

See also: [Wheat § Genetics and breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat#Genetics_and_breeding "Wheat"), and [Wheat taxonomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat_taxonomy "Wheat taxonomy")

Cultivated Einkorn was described as a taxon, *Triticum monococcum*, by [Carl Linnaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus "Carl Linnaeus") in 1753. Later descriptions by other taxonomists, now treated as synonyms, include *Triticum pubescens* by [von Bieberstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_August_Marschall_von_Bieberstein "Friedrich August Marschall von Bieberstein") in 1800; *Triticum hornemanii* by Clementi in 1818; *Nivieria monococcum* in 1841; *Triticum vulgare monococcum* by [Alefeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Georg_Christoph_Alefeld "Friedrich Georg Christoph Alefeld") in 1866; *Triticum monococcum subsp. cereale* by [Albert Thellung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Thellung "Albert Thellung") in 1918.[[8]](#cite_note-Zaharieva_2014-8) Wild Einkorn is known either as *Triticum monococcum* subsp. *aegilopoides* or as *Triticum boeoticum*.[[8]](#cite_note-Zaharieva_2014-8)

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c2/Polyploid_wheat_origins.svg/960px-Polyploid_wheat_origins.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Polyploid_wheat_origins.svg)

Einkorn is related to emmer and bread wheat but is not a hybrid.[[9]](#cite_note-Golovnina-2007-9)

Wild and domesticated einkorns are [diploid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diploid "Diploid") wheats. Unlike [emmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer "Emmer") and [bread wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bread_wheat "Bread wheat"), which were formed by [hybridisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_(biology) "Hybrid (biology)") with *[Aegilops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegilops "Aegilops")* goatgrasses, einkorn is not a hybrid.[[9]](#cite_note-Golovnina-2007-9)

Ecology and distribution
------------------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Einkorn&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Ecology and distribution")]

Einkorn wheat commonly grows wild in the hill country in the northern part of the [Fertile Crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_Crescent "Fertile Crescent") and [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia "Anatolia"), although it has a wider distribution reaching into the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkans "Balkans") and south to [Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan "Jordan") near the [Dead Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Sea "Dead Sea").[[4]](#cite_note-ZoharyHopf3343-4)

History
-------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Einkorn&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: History")]

### Early human use

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Einkorn&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Early human use")]

Einkorn wheat is one of the [earliest cultivated forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_founder_crops "Neolithic founder crops") of wheat, alongside [emmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer "Emmer") wheat (*T. dicoccum*). Hunter gatherers in the [Fertile Crescent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertile_Crescent "Fertile Crescent") may have started harvesting einkorn as early as 30,000 years ago, according to archaeological evidence from Syria.[[10]](#cite_note-10)[[11]](#cite_note-11)[[12]](#cite_note-Allaby-12)

### Domestication

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Einkorn&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Domestication")]

Although gathered from the wild for thousands of years, einkorn wheat was first domesticated approximately 10,000 years BP in the [Pre-Pottery Neolithic A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Pottery_Neolithic_A "Pre-Pottery Neolithic A") (PPNA) or [B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Pottery_Neolithic_B "Pre-Pottery Neolithic B") (PPNB) periods.[[13]](#cite_note-ZoharyHopf38-13) Evidence from [DNA fingerprinting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_fingerprinting "DNA fingerprinting") suggests einkorn was first domesticated near [Karaca Dağ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karaca_Da%C4%9F "Karaca Dağ") in southeast Turkey, an area in which a number of PPNB farming villages have been found.[[14]](#cite_note-Heun1997-14)[Yuval Noah Harari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuval_Noah_Harari "Yuval Noah Harari") suggests that the domestication of einkorn was linked to intensive agriculture to support the nearby [Göbekli Tepe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6bekli_Tepe "Göbekli Tepe") site.[[15]](#cite_note-15)

### Spread of cultivation

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Einkorn&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Spread of cultivation")]

From the northern part of the Fertile Crescent, the cultivation of einkorn wheat spread to the [Caucasus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caucasus "Caucasus"), the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkans "Balkans"), and central Europe. Einkorn wheat was more commonly grown in cooler climates than [emmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer "Emmer") wheat, the other domesticated wheat. Cultivation of einkorn in the Middle East began to decline in favor of emmer wheat around 2000 BCE. Cultivation of einkorn was never extensive in Italy, southern France, and Spain. Einkorn continued to be cultivated in some areas of northern Europe throughout the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages") and until the early part of the 20th century.[[4]](#cite_note-ZoharyHopf3343-4)

Agronomy
--------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Einkorn&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Agronomy")]

Einkorn wheat is low-yielding, but can survive on poor, dry, marginal soils where other varieties of wheat will not. As with other ancient varieties of wheat such as [emmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer "Emmer"), Einkorn is a "covered wheat", as its kernels do not break free from its seed coat ([glume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glume "Glume")) with threshing. This makes it difficult to separate the husk from the seed.[[16]](#cite_note-Stallknecht-16)

Uses
----

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Einkorn&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Uses")]

Einkorn is a common food in northern [Provence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provence "Provence"), France.[[17]](#cite_note-17) It is used for [bulgur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgur "Bulgur") or as [animal feed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_feed "Animal feed") in mountainous areas of countries including France, India, Italy, Morocco, the [former Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Former_Yugoslavia "Former Yugoslavia"), and Turkey.[[16]](#cite_note-Stallknecht-16) It contains [gluten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluten "Gluten") (so is not suitable for people with [gluten-related disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluten-related_disorders "Gluten-related disorders"))[[18]](#cite_note-18) and has a higher percentage of protein than modern red wheats. It is considered more nutritious because it has higher levels of [fat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fat "Fat"), [phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus "Phosphorus"), [potassium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium "Potassium"), [pyridoxine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyridoxine "Pyridoxine"), and [beta-carotene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta-carotene "Beta-carotene").[[16]](#cite_note-Stallknecht-16)

### Use in baking

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Einkorn&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Use in baking")]

Historically, einkorn was not used in breadmaking, having primarily been eaten boiled in whole grains or in porridge[[4]](#cite_note-ZoharyHopf3343-4), meaning that cultivars were not selected for their breadmaking potential.[[19]](#cite_note-:0-19) Until recently, einkorn was not thought to be suitable for baking, as its dough is sticky, inelastic and difficult to handle.[[19]](#cite_note-:0-19) Testing during the 1990s demonstrated that some einkorn varieties could perform favourably in comparison to bread wheats[[20]](#cite_note-:1-20), and, while only some genotypes proved suitable for breadmaking, a wide variety were suitable for use in other baked goods. Einkorn dough has a bright yellow colour[[20]](#cite_note-:1-20) due to high levels of [carotenoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carotenoid "Carotenoid"), and due to low gas retention yields loaves with a dense structure.[[21]](#cite_note-:2-21) Einkorn doughs should be kneaded for a shorter time, and require approximately 10-15 minutes longer baking time than comparable bread wheat doughs.[[21]](#cite_note-:2-21)

### Resource for plant breeding

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Einkorn&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Resource for plant breeding")]

Einkorn is the source of many potential introgressions for immunity; Russian geneticist [Nikolai Vavilov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Vavilov "Nikolai Vavilov") called it an "accumulator of complex immunities".[[8]](#cite_note-Zaharieva_2014-8)*T. monococcum* is the source of *Sr21*, a [stem rust resistance gene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stem_rust_resistance_gene "Stem rust resistance gene") which has been [introgressed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introgressed "Introgressed") into [hexaploid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexaploid_wheat "Hexaploid wheat") worldwide.[[22]](#cite_note-Concepts-and-Methods-22) It is also the source of *Yr34*, a [resistance gene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant_disease_resistance_gene "Plant disease resistance gene") for [yellow rust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat_yellow_rust "Wheat yellow rust").[[23]](#cite_note-Locate_rust_resistance_gene_donated_from_Einkorn-23)

The salt-tolerance feature of *T. monococcum* has been bred into [durum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durum "Durum") wheat.[[24]](#cite_note-24)
