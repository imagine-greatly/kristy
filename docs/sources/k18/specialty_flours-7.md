**Spelt** (*Triticum spelta*), also known as **dinkel wheat**,[[2]](#cite_note-GRIN-2) is a species of [wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat "Wheat"). It is a [relict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relict_(biology) "Relict (biology)") crop, eaten in [Central Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Europe "Central Europe") and northern Spain. It is high in protein. In [Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australia "Australia") it is marketed as a [health food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_food "Health food").

Spelt was cultivated from the [Neolithic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic "Neolithic") period onward. It was a [staple food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staple_food "Staple food") in parts of Europe from the [Bronze Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age_in_Europe "Bronze Age in Europe") to the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"). It is used in baking, and is made into [bread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bread "Bread"), [pasta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasta "Pasta"), and [beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beer "Beer").

It is sometimes considered a subspecies of the closely related [common wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_wheat "Common wheat") (*T. aestivum*), with the botanical name *Triticum aestivum* subsp. *spelta*. It is a [hexaploid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexaploid "Hexaploid"), most likely a hybrid of wheat and [emmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer "Emmer").

Description
-----------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Spelt&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Description")]

Spelt is a species of *[Triticum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triticum "Triticum")*, a large stout grass similar to bread wheat. Its flowering spike is slenderer than that of bread wheat; when ripe, it bends somewhat from the vertical. The spike is roughly four-edged. The axis of the spike is brittle and divided into segments; it shatters into separate segments when fully ripe. Spelt differs from bread wheat in that each seed (a [caryopsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caryopsis "Caryopsis"), botanically a fruit with its wall fused to the single seed inside) stays fully encapsulated by its husk.[[3]](#cite_note-3)

Confusion with other wheats
---------------------------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Spelt&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Confusion with other wheats")]

Especially in the context of descriptions of ancient cultures, the English word *spelt* has sometimes been used for grains that were not *T. spelta*, but other species of hulled wheat such as *T. dicoccum* ([emmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer "Emmer")) or *T. monococcum* ([einkorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einkorn "Einkorn"), also known as "little spelt", in French *petit épeautre*). This confusion may arise either from mistranslation of words found in other languages that can denote hulled wheat in general (such as Italian *[farro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farro "Farro")*, which can denote any of emmer, spelt or einkorn; spelt is sometimes distinguished as *farro grande* ('large farro'),[[4]](#cite_note-Buerli-2006-4) emmer as *farro medio* ('medium farro'),[[4]](#cite_note-Buerli-2006-4) and einkorn as *farro piccolo* ('little farro')[[5]](#cite_note-Cornish-2014-5)), or changing opinions about which actual species of wheat are described in texts written in ancient languages. Thus, the meaning of the ancient Greek word ζειά (*zeiá*) or ζέα is either uncertain or vague, and has been argued to denote einkorn[[6]](#cite_note-6) or emmer rather than spelt.[[7]](#cite_note-7) The ancient Roman grain denoted by the Latin word *far*, although often translated as 'spelt', was in fact emmer.[[8]](#cite_note-8)[[9]](#cite_note-9)

Evolution
---------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Spelt&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Evolution")]

### Hybridisation and polyploidy

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Spelt&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Hybridisation and polyploidy")]

Like common wheat, spelt is a [hexaploid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexaploid "Hexaploid") wheat species, which means it has six sets of [chromosomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromosome "Chromosome"). It is derived from a [hybridisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_(biology) "Hybrid (biology)") event between a domesticated [tetraploid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetraploid "Tetraploid") wheat such as [durum wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durum_wheat "Durum wheat") and another wheat species, increasing the number of sets of [chromosomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromosome "Chromosome").[[10]](#cite_note-Blatter-2004-10) Genetic evidence indicates an initial hybridisation of a domesticated tetraploid wheat and the diploid wild goat-grass *[Aegilops tauschii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aegilops_tauschii "Aegilops tauschii")*. It further shows that spelt could have arisen as the result of a second hybridisation, this time of [bread wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bread_wheat "Bread wheat") and emmer, giving rise to European spelt.[[10]](#cite_note-Blatter-2004-10)[[11]](#cite_note-11) The spelt genome continues to influence the breeding of modern hexaploid bread wheat through recent hybridisation.[[12]](#cite_note-Wang-2024-12)

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/30/Spelt_origins.svg/960px-Spelt_origins.svg.png?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Spelt_origins.svg)

Spelt most likely originated as a [hybrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_(biology) "Hybrid (biology)") of [bread wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bread_wheat "Bread wheat") and [emmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emmer "Emmer"). It continues to influence modern breeds of bread wheat.[[12]](#cite_note-Wang-2024-12)

Spelt, being closely related to bread wheat, is a likely source of [alleles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele "Allele") to increase wheat's genetic diversity, and so improve crop yields. Analysis of the Oberkulmer cultivar of spelt found 40 alleles that could contribute to increased yield. Among the differences were spelt's larger grain size, greater fertility of [tillers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiller_(botany) "Tiller (botany)"), and longer fruiting spikes.[[13]](#cite_note-Xie-2015-13)*Pm5* is an [effector-triggered resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effector-triggered_resistance "Effector-triggered resistance") gene for [powdery mildew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheat_powdery_mildew "Wheat powdery mildew").[[14]](#cite_note-Cowger-2019-14)

### History of cultivation

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Spelt&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: History of cultivation")]

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/2009-06-20_Silvolde_02_dinkel.jpg/250px-2009-06-20_Silvolde_02_dinkel.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2009-06-20_Silvolde_02_dinkel.jpg)

Without and with husks: the husks make spelt suitable for cold climates.[[15]](#cite_note-Newfield_2013-15)

Spelt has been cultivated since approximately 5000 [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era "Common Era"). In the fifth millennium BCE, there are archaeological remains in the north of Iraq and in [Transcaucasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcaucasia "Transcaucasia"), north-east of the [Black Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Sea "Black Sea"). Much more evidence comes from Europe.[[16]](#cite_note-Cubadda-2002-16) Remains of spelt have been found in Denmark, Germany, and Poland from the later Neolithic (dating from 2500–1700 BCE).[[16]](#cite_note-Cubadda-2002-16)[[17]](#cite_note-17) Evidence of spelt has been found from across central Europe from the Bronze Age. In the south of Germany and Switzerland in the Iron Age (750–15 BCE), it was a major type of wheat, while by 500 BCE, it had in addition become widespread in the south of Britain.[[16]](#cite_note-Cubadda-2002-16) There is evidence that spelt cultivation increased in [Iron Age Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Iron_Age "British Iron Age") as damp regions of the country with heavy soils tolerated by spelt were being settled.[[18]](#cite_note-18)

In the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages"), spelt was cultivated in parts of Switzerland, [Tyrol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tyrol_(state) "Tyrol (state)"), Germany, northern France and the southern [Low Countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Countries "Low Countries").[[19]](#cite_note-19) Spelt became a major crop in Europe in the 9th century CE, possibly because it is more suitable for storage and being [husked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hull_(botany) "Hull (botany)") makes it more adaptable to cold climates.[[15]](#cite_note-Newfield_2013-15)

Spelt was introduced to the United States in the 1890s. In the 20th century, spelt was replaced by bread wheat in almost all areas where it was still grown. The [organic farming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organic_farming "Organic farming") movement revived its popularity somewhat toward the end of the 20th century, as spelt requires less [fertilizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fertilizer "Fertilizer").[[20]](#cite_note-20) Since the beginning of the 21st century, spelt has become a common wheat substitute for making [artisanal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artisanal "Artisanal") loaves of bread, pasta, and flakes.[[21]](#cite_note-21) By 2014, the grain was popular in the UK, Kazakhstan, and Ukraine.[[22]](#cite_note-22) In the United States, most spelt is grown in [Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio "Ohio") as of 2022.[[23]](#cite_note-23)[[24]](#cite_note-24)

A 100-gram (3+1⁄2-ounce) reference serving of uncooked spelt provides 1,400 kilojoules (340 kilocalories) of [food energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_energy "Food energy") and is a rich source (20% or more of the [Daily Value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daily_Value "Daily Value")) of [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein "Protein"), [dietary fiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietary_fiber "Dietary fiber"), several [B vitamins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B_vitamins "B vitamins"), and numerous [dietary minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietary_minerals "Dietary minerals") (table). Highest nutrient contents include [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese "Manganese") (143% DV), [phosphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus "Phosphorus") (57% DV), and [niacin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niacin_(nutrient) "Niacin (nutrient)") (46% DV). Spelt contains about 70% total [carbohydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbohydrate "Carbohydrate"), including 11% as dietary fibre, and is low in [fat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fat "Fat") (table).

Spelt contains [gluten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluten "Gluten"), and is therefore suitable for [baking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baking "Baking"), but this component makes it unsuitable for people with [gluten-related disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluten-related_disorders "Gluten-related disorders"), such as [celiac disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celiac_disease "Celiac disease").[[27]](#cite_note-27)
In comparison to hard red [winter wheat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_wheat "Winter wheat"), spelt has a more soluble protein matrix characterized by a higher [gliadin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gliadin "Gliadin"):[glutenin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glutenin "Glutenin") ratio.[[28]](#cite_note-28)[[29]](#cite_note-29)

Products
--------

[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Spelt&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Products")]

In Germany and Austria, spelt loaves and rolls (*Dinkelbrot*) are widely available in bakeries.[[30]](#cite_note-30) The unripe spelt grains are dried and eaten as *[Grünkern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gr%C3%BCnkern "Grünkern")* ("green grain").[[31]](#cite_note-31) In Australia it is grown organically for the [health food](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Health_food "Health food") market.[[32]](#cite_note-32) Dutch *[jenever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jenever "Jenever")* makers sometimes distil with spelt,[[33]](#cite_note-Peragine-2010-33) while [beer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beer "Beer") brewed from spelt exists in [Bavaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavaria "Bavaria")[[34]](#cite_note-34) and Belgium.[[35]](#cite_note-35)
