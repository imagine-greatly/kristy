* What is hummus?

  [Hummus](https://www.britannica.com/topic/hummus/Introduction#ref1) is a paste that comes from the Middle East. It is made from cooked chickpeas that are pureed or mashed and then mixed with tahini, diced garlic, lemon juice, and salt.

  The word hummus comes from the Arabic word for chickpea, *ḥummuṣ*. Hummus is commonly eaten with pita bread or falafel in the Arab world and western Asia. It can be topped with spices or condiments, such as pine nuts, ground beef, or hard-boiled eggs. In the West, it is served as a snack or appetizer with chips, bread, or vegetables. Chickpeas can be rotated with crops such as cereals to improve soil conditions.

* Where did hummus originate?

  Hummus is a paste that originated in the Middle East. It is traditionally made of pureed or mashed cooked chickpeas, tahini, diced garlic, lemon juice, and salt. The word hummus comes from the Arabic word meaning "chickpea".

  Hummus is thought to have originated in the same regions first known to cultivate chickpeas, namely Southwest Asia and its environs. A 13th-century cookbook from Cairo contains the first written record of the food, though without tahini or garlic. Today's version of hummus can be generally attributed to the Middle East and western Asia, with some historians believing that the modern culinary style of hummus was developed for the urban upper class in 18th-century Damascus, Syria.

**hummus**, paste originating in the [Middle East](https://www.britannica.com/place/Middle-East) that is traditionally made of pureed or mashed cooked [chickpeas](https://www.britannica.com/plant/chickpea) mixed with [tahini](https://www.britannica.com/topic/tahini)—a toasted [sesame](https://www.britannica.com/plant/sesame-plant) condiment—and diced [garlic](https://www.britannica.com/plant/garlic), [lemon](https://www.britannica.com/plant/lemon) juice, and [salt](https://www.britannica.com/science/salt). Most of hummus’s nutritional value is derived from the presence of chickpeas, which are a good source of [fibre](https://www.britannica.com/technology/fiber-technology), [protein](https://www.britannica.com/science/protein), [iron](https://www.britannica.com/science/iron-chemical-element), [phosphorus](https://www.britannica.com/science/phosphorus-chemical-element), and [folic acid](https://www.britannica.com/science/folic-acid). Tahini is also rich in phosphorus and iron, as well as [copper](https://www.britannica.com/science/copper) and [selenium](https://www.britannica.com/science/selenium).

The word *hummus* comes from [Arabic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Arabic-language) *ḥummuṣ* (“chickpea”), an abbreviation of the dish’s full name *ḥummuṣ bi-taḥīnah* (“chickpea with tahini”). The word is stylized in [myriad](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/myriad) ways, depending on the language: as *houmos*, *humus*, *hommus*, or *hommos*. In English-language dictionaries, *hummus* first appeared as a defined term between 1950 and 1955, from the [Turkish](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Turkish-language) *humus*.

Throughout the Arab world and parts of western Asia, hummus is commonly eaten fresh with [pita bread](https://www.britannica.com/topic/pita-bread) or [falafel](https://www.britannica.com/topic/falafel). It may be mixed or topped with various spices, like [cumin](https://www.britannica.com/plant/cumin) or [paprika](https://www.britannica.com/topic/paprika), or condiments, such as pine nuts, ground beef, [eggplant](https://www.britannica.com/plant/eggplant), hard-boiled eggs, or zhug (also called skhug; from *saḥāwiq*, a Yemeni [sauce](https://www.britannica.com/topic/sauce) made from green or red chiles). In Mediterranean countries such as [Cyprus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cyprus), Greece, and [Turkey](https://www.britannica.com/place/Turkey), it is considered a meze and served with [bread](https://www.britannica.com/topic/bread). In Israel, Palestine, and [Lebanon](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lebanon), it is a staple [food](https://www.britannica.com/topic/food), often eaten every day, not merely a dip but rather a main dish. It can be found served warm, room temperature, or cold. In the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States) and elsewhere in the West, hummus is found primarily in restaurants or in supermarkets as a packaged, mass-produced food item; it is served as a snack or appetizer to accompany [chips](https://www.britannica.com/topic/French-fries), bread, or fresh vegetables like carrots, celery, or bell peppers.

Hummus is thought by many to have its origins in the same regions first known to [cultivate](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cultivate) chickpeas, namely [Southwest Asia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Southwest-Asia) and its environs. The food’s first appearance in the written record comes from a 13th-century cookbook from [Cairo](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cairo), though this version was notably without tahini or garlic. Some have argued it occurred in writing much earlier, in the Bible: the [Book of Ruth](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Book-of-Ruth) (2:14) contains a passage that reads, “Come hither, and eat of the bread, and dip thy morsel in the *ḥometz*,” although the word refers to [vinegar](https://www.britannica.com/topic/vinegar) elsewhere in the Bible.

While it is ultimately impossible to [ascertain](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/ascertain) the details of hummus’s perhaps ancient history, the form of hummus known today can be generally attributed to the Middle East and western Asia. Some historians conjecture that the modern culinary style of *ḥummuṣ bi-taḥīnah* was developed for the urban upper class in 18th-century [Damascus](https://www.britannica.com/place/Damascus), Syria. The dish’s classic ingredients are staples of these regions as well; lemon, sesame, and garlic have all been [cultivated](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/cultivated) and eaten, on their own or combined, for centuries.

Nonetheless, its origins remain a matter of debate in the Middle East, one clearly rooted in [patriotic](https://www.britannica.com/topic/patriotism-sociology) fervour. In 2008, for example, a Lebanese trade group accused Israel of stealing a Lebanese dish by marketing hummus as Israeli cuisine. The back-and-forth about the food’s ownership between the two countries was dubbed the “Hummus Wars” as both countries vied to set the [Guinness World Record](https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Guinness-Book-of-World-Records) for largest dish of hummus (as of 2010, the record belongs to Lebanese chef Ramzi Choueiri and the students of Al-Kafaàt University in [Beirut](https://www.britannica.com/place/Beirut), who mixed 10,452 kg [23,043 pounds] of hummus). While this competition can come across in [mass media](https://www.britannica.com/topic/mass-media) as [lighthearted](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/lighthearted) one-upmanship, for Arabs the root issue has been cast in the more serious light of geographic tension and [cultural appropriation](https://www.britannica.com/topic/cultural-appropriation). Like the Lebanese, Palestinians also accuse Israelis of not only appropriating hummus’s origins but also its marketability to the West, where, through the success of Israeli brands like Sabra, the savory paste has become increasingly associated with Israel. Hummus production and sales have become massive industries, with a combined global market size exceeding $2.5 billion by 2020.
