## ![](https://www.naturallygrown.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/About-Certifed-Naturally-Grown-1.png)

### What does "Certified Naturally Grown" mean?

CNG producers don’t use any synthetic herbicides, pesticides, fertilizers, or genetically modified organisms. CNG livestock are raised mostly on pasture and with space for freedom of movement. Livestock feed must be grown without synthetic inputs or genetically modified seeds. Production practices are verified with detailed peer-review inspections annually, so the designation requires more rigor than simply making a pledge.

### Why create a whole separate program?

Certified Naturally Grown provides a much-needed complement to the National Organic Program. While the NOP is an important program that primarily serves medium and large-scale agricultural operations, CNG is tailored for direct-market farmers and beekeepers producing food for their local communities. These producers often find the NOP’s heavier paperwork requirements are not a good fit for their small-scale operations. CNG enables them to get credit for their practices while offering accountability to their customers. Some CNG farmers become certified organic after a few years with CNG, and we think that’s just super.

### So how is CNG different than Certified Organic?

CNG is a private non-profit organization that’s not affiliated with the USDA’s National Organic Program. CNG’s certification approach is based on the Participatory Guarantee System (PGS) model that relies on **peer reviews** in which inspections are typically carried out by other farmers. The PGS model promotes farmer-to-farmer knowledge sharing about best practices and fosters local networks that strengthen the farming community. This model **minimizes paperwork** and keeps certification dues **affordable**.

Another difference is that Certified Naturally Grown’s certification process is **transparent** and open to the public. Every CNG producer has a profile on the website. On it you will find the information they submitted in their application, as well as summaries of their inspection reports.

### What is a PGS?

Participatory Guarantee Systems (PGS) are locally-focused quality assurance systems. They certify producers based on active participation of stakeholders and are built on a foundation of trust, social networks and knowledge exchange. Certified Naturally Grown is a Participatory Guarantee System. PGS have existed for decades, but in recent years they have gained recognition for the valuable role they play in the organic movement by including small-scale farmers in organic guarantee systems. [Learn more about PGS](https://www.naturallygrown.org/how-we-operate/)

### Would participation in the CNG program give me permission to use the word organic to describe my farming operation and products?

No. To market your farm products as organic without breaking the law, you’ll need to be certified by an agency that is accredited by USDA’s National Organic Program. Certified Naturally Grown has no formal affiliation with the NOP.

### How should I decide whether to have my farm certified organic or Certified Naturally Grown?

The CNG label is most useful for producers who market directly through CSAs, farmers markets, and local food businesses. If you’re planning to sell to large retail outlets or wholesalers, the National Organic Program might be a better fit. Some farmers choose both certifications (about eight percent of our members), often because they appreciate the local connections and grassroots nature of CNG, but they also need organic certification for access to wholesale organic markets.

### Is your program for farmers who are ‘almost organic,’ but don’t qualify for the USDA Organic Certification?

No! CNG participation requires a full commitment to robust organic practices. Our standards for Produce and Livestock certification are based on the standards of the National Organic Program. We developed from scratch our standards for Apiary, Mushroom, and Aquaponic certifications based on organic principles.

### What are the reasons farmers get CNG certified?

Many farmers are drawn to the opportunity to network with and get feedback from other like-minded farmers. They also want a way to highlight their values and production practices, and gain customer trust through the annual inspections and CNG’s commitment to transparency. Some farmers join for the the marketing advantages of being part of a recognized national organization; markets and local grocers in some areas require that producers be certified. Many consider CNG a good alternative or supplement to being certified organic.

### What ensures that participating farms follow the CNG standards?

Great question!

1. Every certified member must complete an online application describing their production practices. This is reviewed by CNG staff. Once approved, it is posted on the member’s online profile for the public to see.
2. The annual peer inspections also help ensure the program’s integrity.
   - The inspectors are local, such as nearby producers who are using sustainable production methods, and agricultural educators like county Extension Agents. An inspector from the local community is likely to know what’s happening on the certified member’s farm throughout the season.
   - The support networks that develop from these peer inspections help, too. When challenges arise, producers can ask each other for help and avoid situations that might tempt them to cheat because they fear losing a crop.
   - The inspectors who are CNG members have a direct stake in protecting the program’s integrity. Other inspectors must list their farm name or affiliation on the inspection report, which is posted on the member’s profile for the public to see.

### How can I find CNG farms or beekeepers near me?

Just visit [our online listing of CNG members](https://www.naturallygrown.org/directory-of-certified-producers). You can search by state and certification type in the Directory View, or use the Map View to search by zipcode.

### When was CNG started?

CNG was founded by New York farmers in the mid-Hudson Valley in 2002, the same year the National Organic Program took effect.

### Who created your beautiful logo?

The very talented Cecilia Garvin, who was a CSA member of the founding farmers, donated her skills to create the vibrant image that anchors CNG’s marketing materials.

## ![](https://www.naturallygrown.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/FAQSaboutCertifications.png)

### I just started farming! Can I get certified right away?

CNG generally requires that you have experience producing using natural practices before applying for certification. This experience can take place at your own farm/apiary, or a different one.

- There is a lot to learn with any new skill, and the first year is usually filled with trying different practices to find the most successful and sustainable options.

Please reach out if you have questions about this requirement – we’re here to support you! Email [certification@naturallygrown.org](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org)

### This all sounds pretty good to me. How do I get started?

Please review the Certification Types that apply to your operation: [Apiary](https://www.naturallygrown.org/apiary), [Aquaponics](https://www.naturallygrown.org/aquaponics), [Livestock](https://www.naturallygrown.org/livestock), [Mushroom](https://www.naturallygrown.org/mushrooms), [Produce & Flowers](https://www.naturallygrown.org/produce-flowers). If your practices seem like a good fit, then [apply online](https://www.naturallygrown.org/get-certified/). There is no application fee, and you can expect to hear back from us within two weeks.

If you have questions about the standards, request [a free consultation](https://www.naturallygrown.org/consulting) with CNG’s Certification Specialist.

### When should I apply?

We accept applications throughout the year. If you are new to farming, or newly committed to farming without synthetic inputs, then we ask that you not apply until you have at least one year of experience farming this way. Only then can you answer the application questions thoroughly and meaningfully.

### Is there a deadline to apply?

We accept applications throughout the year. To complete the certification process, you’ll need to arrange your on-site inspection while you are in production. Additionally:

- For Produce certification, cover crops or cash crops must be growing.
- For Livestock certification, animals must be present on the farm.
- For Apiary certification, the weather must be suitable for opening the hives.

### What are the program requirements if my application is approved?

There are three core requirements to complete before you’re officially Certified Naturally Grown:

1. Sign the Declaration
2. Pay the Membership Dues
3. Arrange your Inspection

These steps are repeated annually to retain your certification. There is also a “work requirement” where each member must conduct an annual inspection (as long as there is a CNG member who needs an inspection within an hour’s drive). Find details on the [Certification Requirements](https://www.naturallygrown.org/requirements/) page.

### How much does it cost to be a Certified Naturally Grown farmer or beekeeper?

We recommend $300 or more per year, but members have the option to pay less. We offer monthly payment plans to make it easier to pay over time in smaller amounts. Farmers may tap the Grassroots Fund for dues assistance if they’re facing unusual hardship or just getting started. Please visit the [Certification Requirements](https://www.naturallygrown.org/requirements) page for more details.

We _recommend_ $250 or more per year for beekeepers who can afford it, but _there is no minimum dues requirement for apiary certification_, since natural beekeepers help protect honey bees who in turn provide important ecosystem services. We also recognize many beekeepers are hobbyists, deriving only modest income from their apiary. However, CNG beekeepers must pay _some amount_ annually to help keep CNG going strong. We want beekeepers to be able to participate and highlight their commitment to natural practices so more people understand and appreciate why they should be choosy about where they get their honey – and all their food!

### Does it cost more if I have multiple certifications?

No. The membership dues are per operation. For example if you are a Produce grower, you can add Mushroom and Apiary certifications at no additional cost.

### How do I determine if an input is allowed in CNG production?

In order to determine if an input is allowed for use in your CNG operation, go through the following steps.

2. Is the product [OMRI-listed](https://www.omri.org/)?

   - If yes, you’re good to go! (Note that we do not allow rotenone or synthetic hydroponic fertilizer solutions, even though they are OMRI-listed).

   - If no, move onto the next step.

1. Obtain the ingredient list and check that each ingredient is OMRI-listed and/or on the CNG Allowed & Prohibited Substances list for your certification type ( [Apiary](https://www.naturallygrown.org/allowed-and-prohibited-substances-for-cng-apiaries/), [Aquaponics](https://www.naturallygrown.org/document/inputs-materials-allowed-prohibited-for-certified-naturally-grown-aquaponic-produce/), [Livestock & Produce](https://www.naturallygrown.org/allowed-prohibited-inputs/), [Mushrooms](https://www.naturallygrown.org/substrate/).)

1. If every ingredient is allowed, you’re all good!





   - If an ingredient is on the prohibited list, the product is not allowed.
   - If an ingredient is not listed, check with CNG.

Reach out to the [CNG Certification Specialist](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org) with questions.

### Do CNG farmers have to use only organic seeds?

CNG requires that producers use organically grown seeds whenever available. (This applies to all seeds used in the CNG operation including cover crop and pasture seed). The standards grant exceptions for conventionally grown seeds to be used in some cases when a particular variety isn’t available in an organically grown form. We ask that growers make a good faith effort to locate organically grown seeds by contacting at least 3 major suppliers of organic seeds. Please visit the [Seeds & Seedlings](https://www.naturallygrown.org/seed-suppliers/) page for more information.

### What about transplants - do they need to be certified organic?

Transplants must be grown without synthetic fertilizers, wetting agents, or pesticides, and with seeds that meet CNG standards. When purchasing transplants, sometimes it can be difficult to tell how transplants were grown. The best ways to ensure that transplants meet CNG requirements is growing them yourself if you are able, or communicating directly with the transplant grower about their practices. For perennials that were started conventionally, they can be considered fully CNG after 12 months under CNG management. For more details, visit the [Seeds & Seedlings](https://www.naturallygrown.org/seed-suppliers/) page.

### Can I certify some but not all of my crops/products?

While most of our certified producers have their entire operation certified, there are some situations where a farmer hasn’t managed to grow certain crops according to CNG standards (stone fruits or grapes are especially challenging in many areas). Or a farmer may certify their grass-fed cattle but not their pigs due to the lack of suitable feed.

It is possible to exclude some crops or products from certification, and have others be CNG certified. We ask that it be made clear which is which during marketing, and that this is explained clearly on the farm’s public profile on the CNG website.

### What is the transitional period for CNG producers?

For farmers or beekeepers who are interested in certification but have a history of chemical use on their land:

- CNG specifies a 36-month transitional period which begins on the last application of synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, or herbicides.
- Unlike the National Organic Program, CNG allows the producer to be certified during this transitional period \*:
  - The producer’s public profile and application highlight the transitional period and the reason for it.
  - The producer is considered fully certified during this time and has full membership benefits including access to marketing materials, use of the CNG logo, marketing their products as CNG, etc.

This is CNG’s way of balancing full information and transparency for customers, while also supporting and recognizing producers that switch to natural practices.

\\* Please reach out to our [Certification Specialist](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org) to discuss your situation.

### How do I label processed foods and value-added products?

CNG certifies raw agricultural products, and minimally processed products like honey and maple syrup. We’ve set the following policies to address questions about labeling from members who create value-added products.

- The CNG logo may only be used on products where the majority of inputs by volume are CNG certified ingredients. Common examples are sauerkraut, pickles, jams, teas, and meat.
- If the majority of ingredients are not CNG, then the CNG logo may not be placed on the packaging, but the member has the option to indicate which ingredients are Certified Naturally Grown. For example, a tomato sauce where only the basil is CNG, but not the tomatoes, may not use the logo but the ingredient list may indicate Certified Naturally Grown basil, or use an asterisk to indicate which ingredients are CNG certified.

Some value-added products require major ingredients that aren’t available in CNG certified form, like oils and alcohol, in which case there’s more flexibility with using the CNG logo.

- Tinctures: May use the CNG logo if the majority of plant ingredients are CNG, and the alcohol is certified organic.
- Salves and Infused Oils: May use the CNG logo if the plant ingredients are CNG, and the oils are certified organic. Honey and beeswax should also be from a CNG apiary.
- If a particular plant ingredient is sometimes organic, and sometimes CNG, then the specific situation should be discussed with CNG. If a particular plant ingredient is sometimes CNG and sometimes conventional, then the ingredient may **not** be noted as sometimes CNG.

### Can I be certified if I'm not selling anything?

Yes. We do have members who are:

- Non-profits who donate all their produce
- Schools who are using the certification as an educational tool
- Conservation landowners who need some type of certification to verify their land care practices

Please [reach out](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org) to discuss your situation so we can help guide you!

### Why don't you certify hydroponic operations?

CNG has chosen to diverge from the National Organic Program (NOP) and does not certify hydroponic operations, in large part because:

- The NOP allows the use of OMRI-approved synthetic fertilizers for hydroponic systems. CNG prohibits these products.

- Most hydroponic operations we’ve encountered do not use natural fertility sources. So at this time we cannot justify taking the time to develop meaningful standards for hydroponics.

### Does CNG certify Hemp or Cannabis?

CNG policy is that we do not certify recreational Marijuana operations. However, we will certify Hemp with THC levels lower than 0.3%. For producers wanting to label their Hemp oil, the producer can market the oil as “made from CNG Hemp” but the oil itself cannot be certified. Please see the [CNG Value-Added Guidance](https://www.naturallygrown.org/considerations-for-value-added-producers/) for more detail regarding labeling or contact the [CNG certification specialist](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org).

### How does certification work for farmer-training programs?

It depends on how the program is organized.

The primary question is: Who makes the final decision about input purchases and production practices?

- If there is one manager who makes these decisions for all the farmers-in-training, then the program can be certified as a single farm. \*
- If the farmers-in-training make their own choices AND there is a formal process where the manager reviews those choices before proceeding, then the program can be certified as a single farm. \*
- If the farmers-in-training are fully responsible for choosing their own inputs and practices, then the individual farmers are considered to be managing their own farms and must seek certification separately.

\\* In these situations, the manager’s name should be listed on the CNG account, and they should fill out and submit the farm’s application. All products that the farm produces in this manner, and which follow CNG standards, can be marketed as CNG.

CNG is eager to support beginning farmers on their journey in sustainable agriculture. Please reach out and tell us about your program and any challenges you’re facing, and we’ll be happy to work with you.

### How do I submit an inspection report?

You can submit the report electronically by emailing it to CNG at forms@naturallygrown.org. Any format is fine as long as we can read it 🙂 including:

- a scanned copy
- pictures of the pages
- or, if your email server is balking at the file size: upload the file or images to an online folder such as Google or iCloud Drive, and share the folder with us

Or you can mail the paper copy to CNG, PO Box 153, Temple, NH 03084

### How can I find CNG farms or beekeepers near me?

Just visit [our online listing of CNG members](https://www.naturallygrown.org/directory-of-certified-producers/). You can search by state and certification type in the [Directory View](https://www.naturallygrown.org/directory-of-certified-producers/), or use the [Map View](https://www.naturallygrown.org/directory-of-certified-producers/#map-view) to search by zipcode.

The listing is always up to date with currently certified members.

### What if there are no CNG producers near me?

First, if you’re not sure who’s eligible to conduct your inspection, refer to the [Inspection Guidelines](https://www.naturallygrown.org/inspection-forms/) for your certification type. Here are the most common types of inspectors other than CNG members:

- A farmer or beekeeper who’s using natural practices and is experienced with your type of production. They might be certified Organic, or they may have no certification. Consider the vendors at your market, or producers in your local farming or beekeeping networks.
- Sustainable agriculture educators: Check with your local colleges and universities. Inspections make great field trips for students! \*\*
- Agricultural service providers: In the US check with your county Extension Agent or NRCS Agent; also consider Soil & Water Conservation District staff. In Canada check with your Regional Agrologists and Conservation Officers. The inspection worksheets cover the same kinds of topics that these professionals would discuss during a standard farm visit, so inspections are usually considered as part of their mission. \*\*

\\*\\* Non-profit educators/service providers only; this excludes for-profit landscape/agricultural businesses which might have a conflict of interest.

When you’re reaching out to someone who isn’t familiar with CNG inspections and is concerned that they’re not qualified, you can assure them that:

- It is CNG’s responsibility to determine whether a farm is adhering to the standards.
- The job of the inspector is to be CNG’s “eyes on the ground” – asking the questions on the inspection worksheets, and noting any questions they have.
- You can always refer them to CNG and we’ll be happy to answer their questions and concerns.

Please [reach out to CNG](mailto:forms@naturallygrown.org) for additional information, or anytime you’d like help finding an inspector.

### Can I repeat an inspector?

The answer is generally, Yes. However there is one special case (see \* below):

- CNG encourages you to connect with a variety of inspectors because you’ll both benefit from each others’ knowledge and experiences. However we understand that in some areas this is difficult because there aren’t many options. Refer to the [Inspection Guidelines](https://www.naturallygrown.org/inspection-forms/) if you need a refresher on who’s eligible to conduct your inspection, or check out [Inspections: Who, What, When & How](https://www.naturallygrown.org/all-about-inspections/) for a deeper dive.
- \\* When you use the same inspector 2 years in a row, you’ll also need to invite a [Community Observer](https://www.naturallygrown.org/include-an-observer/) to add integrity to the inspection process. The observer cannot be related to or employed by you or the inspector, but they can be a customer, an aspiring farmer or beekeeper, a science teacher, a baker or florist, a neighbor, a reporter….

### Can I inspect more than one CNG producer this year?

This is a terrific question! You definitely -can- however keep in mind that you’ll need someone to inspect your own operation. CNG rules prohibit trading inspections until 2 years have passed. Consider:

– When there’s a group of CNG producers in a region, we encourage you to work together to inspect each others’ operations \[following the [inspection guidelines](https://www.naturallygrown.org/inspection-forms/) about who is eligible to inspect who.\] The trading problem is easily avoided if there are 4 or more producers. If there are fewer than 4, then you can add alternatives into the mix such as Extension Agents, remote inspections, 3-customer inspections, etc.

– With that said, if you’re struggling to find an inspector because you’ve done multiple inspections in the past year, please [reach out](mailto:forms@naturallygrown.org) and we’ll be happy to work with you!

### What if I have multiple locations for my farm/apiary?

During your first inspection, each location needs to be visited. The inspector will fill out just **one set of worksheets**, and they can make note of any practices, buffers, etc that differ between the sites.

For future inspections, you can alternate between the locations. But regardless of which location you’re physically visiting, your answers to the inspection questions should include all your locations.

### Can a Master Gardener inspect my farm?

No – if the person’s only affiliation is “Master Gardener.”

Yes – if they are also a market farmer who is using natural practices. They should list their farm name as their affiliation on the inspection report.

In general, Master Gardeners are not eligible to conduct inspections because they don’t have the experience and skills gained from commercial production. Also, Extension programs in some states only teach conventional growing methods, so a Master Gardener may have no knowledge of sustainable practices.

### Can my Farmers Market Manager inspect my farm?

No. There is an inherent conflict of interest because the market benefits from your farm being certified.

### Can I pay the inspector?

The built-in value of CNG’s peer reviews is the sharing of knowledge between the producer and the inspector. No money changes hands. Instead, the producer and inspector learn from each other as they work through the inspection forms – discussing their challenges, sharing their observations and experiments, and brainstorming solutions.

- When asking a CNG producer for an inspection: Every CNG member commits to inspecting another CNG operation annually (when there’s one within an hour’s drive.) So you all share equally in the work.
- When asking someone who isn’t a CNG producer for an inspection: We understand that this is a more difficult ask. However we often receive happy notes from these inspectors, excited that they learned something new during the inspection to try on their own farm. And sometimes the relationships built through inspections grow and expand into collaborations down the road. If you feel you must offer something, perhaps arrange to visit the inspector’s farm to help for a couple of hours – and you might learn something new from them!

### Are you hiring inspectors?

Certified Naturally Grown is a Participatory Guarantee System and as such relies on peer-review inspections instead of third-party inspections. We do not hire inspectors. Inspections are typically conducted by local CNG producers.

### How do I determine if an input is allowed in CNG production?

In order to determine if an input is allowed for use in your CNG operation, go through the following steps.

2. Is the product [OMRI-listed](https://www.omri.org/)?





   - If yes, you’re good to go! (Note that we do not allow rotenone or synthetic hydroponic fertilizer solutions, even though they are OMRI-listed).
   - If no, move onto the next step.

1. Obtain the ingredient list and check that each ingredient is OMRI-listed and/or on the CNG Allowed & Prohibited Substances list for your certification type ( [Apiary](https://www.naturallygrown.org/allowed-and-prohibited-substances-for-cng-apiaries/), [Aquaponics](https://www.naturallygrown.org/document/inputs-materials-allowed-prohibited-for-certified-naturally-grown-aquaponic-produce/), [Livestock & Produce](https://www.naturallygrown.org/allowed-prohibited-inputs/), [Mushrooms](https://www.naturallygrown.org/substrate/).)

1. If every ingredient is allowed, you’re all good! But…





   - If an ingredient is on the prohibited list, the product is not allowed.
   - If an ingredient is not listed, check with CNG.

Reach out to the [CNG Certification Specialist](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org) if you still have questions.

### Is the label "For Organic Gardening" the same as OMRI?

Liquid fertilizers, pesticides, fungicides, and herbicides should, in general, have the OMRI label if they are to be used on a CNG farm. Confusion is common with these products because labeling language can be misleading. A few pest control products like 100% Neem oil may contain just one approved substance, but most are formulations with an active ingredient and additional inactive ingredients. Usually only the active ingredient is listed on the label and companies are reticent to provide full ingredient lists.

> Many garden product companies, like Bonide and Fox Farm, make products that have the same active ingredient as OMRI-listed products, but are listed for organic **gardening** and do not go through the OMRI verification process.

Sometimes the rest of the ingredients in these products are fine, but other times they contain petroleum products or other synthetic materials. To confuse matters further, some of these companies sell both organic and conventional products, with very similar product design and marketing language. Be sure you look for an OMRI label or obtain a **full list** of ingredients from the manufacturer before using a pest or disease control product.

### How do I know if the compost I buy is ok to use in CNG?

CNG does not require that compost be OMRI-listed, we just ask you to verify with the supplier that the compost is:

- Properly composted if it contains animal manures (i.e. they are monitoring the C:N ratio, temperatures, and turning schedule)
- Not made from biosolids
- Free from prohibited additions of synthetic fertilizers

Just a simple phone call or email to the supplier is usually sufficient.

### I use manure on my farm. What are the application guidelines?

Like our compost standards, [CNG’s manure requirements](https://www.naturallygrown.org/produce-flowers-standards/#soilfert) are based on National Organic Program, [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/types-composting-and-understanding-process#:~:text=In%20aerated%20static%20pile%20composting,the%20top%20of%20the%20pile.), and FDA guidelines for food safety. For applications of raw or partially composted manure, we require the following intervals between manure application and crop harvest:

- 120 day interval for crops in which the edible portion is in contact with the soil (i.e. carrots, lettuce, radishes, bush beans, etc.)
- 90 day interval for crops in which the edible portion is not in contact with the soil (i.e. trellised tomatoes, fruit trees, sweet corn, pole beans, etc.)

No waiting period is required for non-edible crops like cut flowers, hay, and cover crops.

### My manure pile has aged for several years so it's fully composted, right?

Not necessarily! The [composting standards](https://www.naturallygrown.org/produce-flowers-standards/#soilfert) for manure must be followed (C:N ratio, turning, temperatures), otherwise the manure is considered “raw” even if it ages for twenty years.

Since reading the standards is a bit dry, we’ve written this fun and practical [blog post](https://www.naturallygrown.org/compost-manure-and-the-in-between/) that explains all you need to know.

### I make my compost on-farm. Is this a problem?

[CNG’s compost standards](https://www.naturallygrown.org/produce-flowers-standards/#soilfert) are based on National Organic Program, [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sustainable-management-food/types-composting-and-understanding-process#:~:text=In%20aerated%20static%20pile%20composting,the%20top%20of%20the%20pile.), and FDA guidelines for food safety. If a farmer wants to apply farm-produced compost that includes manure, they’ll need to monitor the initial C:N ratio, take temperature readings, and turn the pile often to ensure that their compost is properly finished.

It’s very common that farmers with smaller compost systems cannot meet the compost requirements, since it’s difficult to maintain temperatures in smaller piles. In this case, farmers just apply their compost by following the 90/120 “manure rule” explained at the above link, or apply it only to non-produce crops (hay, landscape, pasture, cover crop, etc.).

### I make compost tea on my farm. What do I need to know?

Compost tea is a common input on CNG farms, but a few considerations should be made when brewing and applying.

- If the tea is made with finished compost and/or vermicompost and contains no additives, it can be applied without restriction.
- The addition of fish emulsion or other additives like molasses, kelp, unfinished compost, etc. increases the risk of e. coli, listeria, and other harmful bacteria.
  - If the additives **do not** include raw manure or unfinished compost containing manure, and the tea is applied **within an hour of mixing,** the tea can be applied without restriction.
  - Otherwise, CNG requires the tea be applied with same restrictions as raw manure (i.e. following the [90/120 rule](https://www.naturallygrown.org/faq/i-use-manure-on-my-farm-what-are-the-application-guidelines/)).

### Are other farm-made inputs allowed?

CNG encourages farmers to produce their own inputs, including those used in Korean Natural Farming (KNF) and JADAM, as a way to reduce costs and have more control over what goes into them.

\\* CNG may restrict the application window for these inputs depending on the ingredients used, particularly when animal-based products are included. Please [reach out](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org) to us with the list of ingredients you’re using and the desired method of application, and we’ll be happy to dig into the details.

### Is rooting hormone allowed?

Rooting hormones are not allowed in CNG production unless they are OMRI-listed. Most hormones are synthetically derived and are not in line with the National Organic Program either. There are few products that really compare to the synthetic rootinghormones, though folks have had luck with endo and ecto mycorrhizal fungi products like [EZ Root](https://www.arbico-organics.com/product/e-z-root-rooting-compound-organic/propagation-tools-supplies). [FOOP Organic Cloning Gel](https://thefoop.com/products/foop-clone-gel-4oz) is another option.

## ![](https://www.naturallygrown.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Apiary-FAQ.png)

### I just started keeping bees. Can I get my apiary CNG certified?

No matter how enthusiastic a beginning beekeeper may be about using natural practices, the learning curve is very steep. We don’t accept applications from beekeepers with less than one year of experience. Instead, we encourage you to review and rely on our [Handbook for Natural Beekeeping](https://www.naturallygrown.org/natural-beekeeping-resources/) as you set out to keep bees as naturally as possible. Note we recommend you not harvest any honey from your first-year apiary. The bees will need it all!

### How can you be sure the honey bees only touch organic land and crops?

You can’t! That’s why the management practices of beekeepers is so important.

### There aren't any CNG beekeepers near me. Can I participate? How would I get inspected?

While we recommend you rely on a CNG beekeeper if you can, it often isn’t possible to find one within an hour’s drive. So instead you may rely on a beekeeper in your area who uses natural methods, even if they’re not part of Certified Naturally Grown.

Another option is to invite 3 customers or community members to conduct your inspection. This is the perfect opportunity to explain how much we depend on the bees, and how everyone can help.

### Are beekeepers required to pay the full certification dues if it's just a small operation?

There is no minimum dues requirement for Apiary certification. We recommend $300 per year for those who can afford it, but we only require that each CNG beekeeper give some amount. We recognize that natural beekeepers are playing an important role protecting honey bees, and very few are deriving any significant income from their apiary. We want beekeepers to be able to participate and highlight their commitment to natural practices so more people understand and appreciate why they should be choosy about where they get their honey – and all their food!

## ![](https://www.naturallygrown.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Aquaponics-FAQ.png)

### What is aquaponics?

Aquaponics combines aquaculture (raising fish or other aquatic animals) with hydroponics (growing plants in water) in a bacteria-mediated symbiotic, closed-loop, system. The waste produced by aquatic animals is converted by beneficial bacteria into nutrients that the plants can absorb. In turn, the plants purify the water before it is returned to the fish.

### What are the benefits of aquaponics?

- It uses 90% less water than soil-based farming

- It can produce fresh, local produce and fish year-round

- It produces food reliably without the use of synthetic chemicals.

- Local food can be produced in places where soil-based farming is extremely difficult or unwise, such as where there is drought, soil contamination, or high-density urban spaces.

### Doesn’t aquaponics require a lot more plastic than traditional soil-based farms?

Many of the components in aquaponics systems are plastic. However, they are generally reusable and have a long useful life. Soil-based farms also routinely rely on a lot of plastic products (pots, landscape fabric or mulch, drip tape, irrigation pipe, twine), which tend to have a relatively short useful life and are discarded annually, or bi-annually.

### Are the plants, the fish, or both certified? Why?

Just the plants will be CNG certified. There is only a very small supply of fish feed that would meet CNG standards for livestock certification, and that supply of organic fish feed doesn’t contain fish meal, an ingredient which professional producers consider essential to ensure palatability. That and other features of aquaponic fish management lead to the decision to postpone CNG certification for aquaponic fish until a later date.

### Who can conduct Aquaponics inspections?

Eligible types of inspectors include:

- CNG Aquaponics producers
- CNG Produce & Flower growers \*
- Certified Organic producers \*
- Non-certified producers who are using natural methods \*

\\* For soil-based growers who are not familiar with Aquaponics systems, please refer them to [this overview](https://www.naturallygrown.org/document/aquaponics-inspections-guidance-for-soil-based-farmers/) of how the system works.

For full details on inspector options, see the [Inspection Guidelines for Aquaponics](https://www.naturallygrown.org/document/inspection-guidelines-aquaponics/).

### Who decides whether the producer’s system is “designed to adequately to perform certain functions that are critical to the overall health of an aquaponic system” (e.g., bio filtration, removal of fish waste solids, water circulation, aeration, degassing), and why aren’t these standards more specific?

Since Certified Naturally Grown does not require any particular design or kind of system, it is not possible to set very specific standards for assessing whether a system is performing functions critical to the overall health of the system. There are just too many variables. However, CNG does require producers to ensure that their aquaponic system is designed to adequately perform these essential functions. In the application process, producers will be required to describe in their own words how their system is designed to perform these functions.  A preliminary assessment will be based on the answers they provide initially, and in subsequent correspondence. Further assessment will be made by the inspector during an on-site review. During this review, recommendations may be made for system improvement. Indeed, this is one of the benefits of our peer-review system! But, if there is disagreement between producer and inspector about what is adequate, the case may be taken up by CNG’s Aquaponics Advisory Council, if there isn’t precedent, or by CNG staff, if the Council has already addressed the particular question.

### You require producers employ practices that prevent fish disease. What factors impact fish health?

Factors impacting fish health include the following: handling, transport, and harvesting practices; temperature, pH, light levels and stocking densities; feed type and quantity; water quality, filtration and dissolved oxygen; biosecurity and hygiene.

### Does CNG define any standards relating to fish feed?

We encourage producers to use feed that is organically grown, and feed that does not contain primary catch. However, because the organic fish feed market is in its early stages, there is limited availability and an extremely high cost barrier. Therefore we do not require that fish feed be organic, and we will not offer CNG certification for the fish raised in a CNG operation. As organic fish feed becomes more available, we will revisit CNG fish feed standards. CNG draft standards prohibit fish feed that contains (or is) medications, hormones, or human or other animal waste.

### Why do you allow but discourage vinyl? And EPDM?

We are aware of concerns about off gassing and leaching, but there isn’t conclusive scientific evidence that would justify prohibiting them. These materials may be prohibited in the future, pending more evidence.

### Why is recycled plastic not allowed?

Recycled plastics present an increased rate of degradation and risk of leaching compared to virgin plastic, and additional risks posed by chemicals that may be added to plastics shipped overseas for recycling.

### Do you allow the use of IBC totes?

In response to public comments, we made some changes to the draft standards. It is prohibited for IBC totes to be brought into an already CNG-certified operation. However, recognizing that many start-ups use IBC totes, the revised standards will allow IBC totes to be _grandfathered in_ to CNG operations, but ONLY IF a) they were in use by that operation prior to certification, and b) they were either purchased new or it can be verified that they were only used for food-grade purposes prior to use by the aquaponics operation. It is expected that the grandfathered IBC totes will eventually be replaced. CNG has prohibited the introduction of IBC totes to a CNG aquaponics operation because they are designed for a one-way trip, not continuous use, so they are subject to degradation – just like water bottles and milk jugs – and shouldn’t be relied upon for long-term use in a commercial aquaponics operation where food is being produced for sale to others.

### Do you allow soil, compost, or vermicompost in aquaponics systems?

In response to public comments, we updated the standards to allow for soil, compost, and vermicompost to be used in dead-end or non-recirculating sections of the system. These materials are natural, non-synthetic sources of nutrients. Many aquaponics systems function perfectly well without the use of these materials, but some producers find soil, compost, and vermicompost useful. CNG’s revised AP standards will allow their use in CNG operations, as long as the water does not recirculate from the portion of the system where those materials are used.

### What pH adjustment products are allowed?

Minimally processed forms of calcium carbonate are allowed such as dolomitic lime and oyster shell flour. However, most pH buffering materials are synthetic, and at this time most producers consider them necessary to achieve a steady and balanced pH without causing stress for fish and the system as a whole. CNG allows these synthetic materials (such as calcium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, and others), at least until further developments in the field of aquaponics yields a more natural alternative.

### Are compressed grow plugs or rock wool allowed?

They are allowed as long as they don’t contain synthetic fertilizers. These are products where fibers (peat or coir in the case of most grow plugs, and mineral fibers in the case of rock wool) are compressed and held together with binding materials. The structure provides a porous growing medium that provides structural support to plant roots while allowing the right mix of air and water to reach the roots.  Certified Naturally Grown views these products as essentially serving the same function that plastic pots serve on soil-based farms.

## ![](https://www.naturallygrown.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Livestock-FAQ.png)

### How can I tell if my feed qualifies?

- If your feed is certified organic, then it meets CNG standards.
- If your livestock are pasture-raised and don’t receive supplemental feed then you’re probably good to go, so long as you don’t add synthetic fertilizers to your own fields!
- If you buy from a local supplier, we recommend you show them the CNG [Feed Form](https://www.naturallygrown.org/inspection-forms/) (required of all CNG livestock producers) and see if they can affirm those requirements are met.

If you are having difficulty locating feed that meets CNG standards, you might get some leads from this preliminary [list of feed suppliers](https://www.naturallygrown.org/feed-suppliers/).

### Will my non-GMO feed meet CNG requirements?

It is not sufficient for the feed to be non-GMO. That’s important, but the feed ALSO must be grown without synthetic fertilizers, herbicides, or pesticides. If your feed is marketed as non-GMO, but not certified organic, then it very likely is grown with synthetic inputs.

### How soon can I certify my livestock after switching to an approved feed?

For farmers who are new to CNG and switching their livestock to a feed that meets the CNG standards, there is a transition period of 6 months.

For other questions about transitioning livestock, please contact our [Certification Specialist](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org).

### Is there a pasture requirement?

All animals must have access to the outdoors, as well as shade, shelter, fresh air, and direct sunlight, except in cases of inclement weather. In particular, ruminants should spend most of their time on pasture for 120 days or more during the growing season.

### What are the minimum housing or space requirements for poultry?

Poultry must have at least 5 square feet per bird of free-roaming space outdoors, or 2 square feet per bird if poultry is penned and is moved to fresh pasture each day. When kept indoors overnight or during extreme weather, they should have at least 1.75 square feet per bird.

### Can I use treated wood in my operation?

Treated lumber can be used for new construction of farm infrastructure as long as there is an appropriate buffer between the installation and your livestock grazing/sheltering area. Because each operation is laid out uniquely, please [contact CNG](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org) to discuss the recommended buffer zone based on your situation.

Existing treated wood can be grandfathered in.

If you’re looking for alternatives, you might consider naturally rot-resistant wood, such as cedar, black locust, black walnut, or white oak; materials like concrete, plastic or steel; or wood-plastic composites, as long as they do not contain fungicides.

### Can I certify some but not all the livestock on my farm?

Yes, just be clear on your application about which animals you’re wanting to get certified, and which will be excluded from certification. You will need to submit a Feed Form for each type of livestock for which you seek certification, and for each of your feed suppliers.

### Are vaccines allowed?

Yes! We absolutely encourage CNG farmers and ranchers to use vaccines as part of their disease management plan. Since most conventional veterinary medications are not allowed, having a robust strategy for managing herd and flock health is key.

### What if one of my animals gets sick?

Most conventional veterinary medicines are not allowed under CNG standards (vaccines are an important exception). When necessary to prevent animal suffering and death, CNG requires producers to use the most effective veterinary treatment available –whether synthetic or natural. Preserving certification status is not an acceptable reason to withhold needed medical treatment. However, if synthetic treatments are used, the animal cannot be marketed as CNG and should be removed from the flock or herd.

### What should I avoid if I'd like to get my mushroom operation CNG certified?

You’ll want to be very careful about your choice of substrate; it can’t include byproducts of genetically engineered agricultural crops (like most cottonseed hulls and soy meal). You’ll need to have a clean water source, too. Review the standards to see if they’re a good fit with your practices, and get in touch if you have any questions.

### Why have separate standards for mushrooms and produce?

Mushrooms are not grown in soil and behave differently than plants. Separate standards were needed to provide more meaningful guidance for production of this distinct type of crop.

### I grow microgreens. What do I need to know?

Please review our [Top Tips for Microgreens Growers](https://www.naturallygrown.org/top-tips-for-microgreens/) page. If you have any questions or concerns, please [reach out.](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org)

### I'm a flower grower. What do I need to know?

Please review our [**Considerations for Flower Growers**](https://www.naturallygrown.org/considerations-for-flower-growers/) documentation. If you have any questions or concerns, please [reach out](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org).

### What about potting mix? Is there anything I should look for?

To meet CNG standards, potting mix must not contain synthetic ingredients such as chemical fertilizers or synthetic wetting agents. Commercial potting mixes frequently contain one or both of these, so it’s important to check the ingredient list carefully if you purchase potting mix. If you are able, making your own potting mix gives you more control over the ingredients. It’s easiest to purchase OMRI-listed potting mix, since obtaining ingredient lists from large companies can be tricky and sometimes fruitless. However, there are many smaller, regional companies that make perfectly great potting mix with readily available, acceptable ingredient lists. Like sourcing any input, if the potting mix you want to buy is not OMRI-listed, just make sure that you’ve checked the **full** ingredient list for any [prohibited inputs](https://www.naturallygrown.org/allowed-prohibited-inputs/).

### What practices can I use to increase soil fertility?

To manage fertility on their farm, CNG farmers use management practices like cover crops and crop rotations, and organic amendments such as compost, manure, fish emulsion, soybean meal, feather and bone meal, and unprocessed minerals. Synthetic fertilizers are not allowed.

### What can I use to control weeds, pests, and diseases?

CNG encourages mechanical and cultural methods as the first line of defense against weeds, pests, and diseases. When those methods don’t solve the issue, most botanical and biological controls, such as ladybugs, Bt, Neem, or Kayolin clay, are also allowed. (Rotenone is a notable exception; though it is an unprocessed botanical, it has been linked to Parkinson’s Disease). As a general rule, most OMRI-approved products meet CNG standards. No synthetic herbicides, pesticides, or fungicides are allowed.

### I'm dealing with a micronutrient deficiency. What are my CNG-approved options?

Synthetic micronutrients may be used:

- only for fertility, **and**
- they must be OMRI-listed **, and**
- there must be a documented deficiency in the form of a soil or tissue test.

This applies to the following nutrients:

- Boron
- Zinc
- Copper
- Iron
- Manganese
- Molybdenum
- Selenium
- Cobalt

### I buy my onion starts from my favorite seed company who sells tons of organic seed. The onion starts don't say that they're organic, but I can still use them, right?

No! All annual transplants must be grown according to CNG standards. So either you grow them on your farm, or you purchase them from an organic or CNG source. If a plant or seed is organic, the company will tell you all about it, with clear labeling on their website or catalogue. If you cannot tell if the onion plants you want are organic, they are very likely NOT organic. There are very few suppliers of organic onion transplants so check your sources carefully. Most CNG farmers grow their own onion transplants or contract other CNG or organic growers to produce them. Check out our [Planting Stock](https://www.naturallygrown.org/source-cng-planting-stock/) and [Seeds & Seedlings](https://www.naturallygrown.org/seed-suppliers/) pages or more details.

### I grow day-neutral strawberries and I replant them every year. I can't find organic planting slips on my scale. What should I do?

Day-neutral strawberries grown as annuals are covered under the seed sourcing rule. So, like with seeds, CNG producers must check with at least three suppliers to ensure that organic plants are not available, before purchasing conventional plants. This exception does not apply to strawberries grown as perennials. See the [Seeds & Seedlings](https://www.naturallygrown.org/seed-suppliers/) page for more details. This [CCOF article](https://www.ccof.org/article/organic-strawberry-planting-stock-field-trials-and-commercial-availability-requirement) also has some helpful strawberry sourcing guidance.

### Organic seed potatoes are expensive and the organic sources are far away, making freight really expensive too! Can I use non-organic seed potatoes from a source closer to me?

Seed potatoes are covered under the seed standard, which means that a CNG grower must use organic seed potatoes, unless the variety and/or quantity they need is unavailable in an organic form. Cost is not a justification to purchase conventional seed or planting stock. Most common potato varieties are available organically from many suppliers. Though organic potato seed is more expensive and shipping can be burdensome, remember that organic potato seed was produced in an organic production system and will be more adapted to the conditions on your farm! Check out our [Planting Stock](https://www.naturallygrown.org/source-cng-planting-stock/) and [Seeds & Seedlings](https://www.naturallygrown.org/seed-suppliers/) pages or more details.

### I want to grow ginger and turmeric. Can I just buy some from the grocery store and get started?

Ginger and turmeric (just like garlic, seed potatoes, and flower bulbs) are covered under the seed standard, so CNG farmers must purchase organic ginger and turmeric roots, unless the variety and/or quantity is not available organically. Unless you have an extremely large operation, this exception will not apply to you and you’ll need to source organic planting stock.

### How big do my buffers need to be?

It is important for CNG farmers to have adequate buffers around their growing areas to protect them from potential sources of contamination. Consider buffers from neighboring properties, livestock areas, manure piles (or manure-based compost piles), and adjacent areas where prohibited products may be applied or sprayed (i.e. herbicides or pesticides).

The appropriate size of the buffer depends on the context, including factors like prevailing wind patterns, the elevation and slope of the land, and the neighboring land uses. Typically, we look for a minimum distance of 20 feet to crops like a fertilized hayfield, field corn and soybeans, while a buffer of 50 feet is required for crops such as sweet corn. A buffer may need to be somewhat larger if for example the growing area is located downhill or downwind, or it may need to be quite a bit larger if you are located next to crops that are sprayed aerially.

### Can I use treated wood in my operation?

Treated lumber can be used for new construction of farm infrastructure as long as there is an appropriate buffer between the installation and your production area. Because each operation is laid out uniquely, please [contact CNG](mailto:certification@naturallygrown.org) to discuss the recommended buffer zone based on your situation.

Existing treated wood can be grandfathered in.

If you’re looking for alternatives, you might consider naturally rot-resistant wood, such as cedar, black locust, black walnut, or white oak; materials like concrete, plastic or steel; or wood-plastic composites, as long as they do not contain fungicides.

### Do you allow the Paper Pot Transplanter?

Yes, we consider the paper chain pots to be compostable, in the same category as paper mulches. Learn more about [our stance on paper pot transplant](https://www.naturallygrown.org/paper-pot-transplanter/) [ers](https://www.cngfarming.org/paper_pot_transplanter) and [biofilm](https://www.naturallygrown.org/plastic-mulch-film/).

### I make jams and salsas from my CNG produce. Can I use the CNG label on my products?

The CNG label may be used on packaging only if the following two conditions are met:

2. It states clearly on the label which ingredients are CNG certified (for example, “made with Certified Naturally Grown tomatoes” or “Ingredients: tomatoes\*, onions, basil\*, olive oil, salt, pepper   \*Certified Naturally Grown ingredients”), AND

1. At least one of the two main ingredients is CNG (so for example, the label could not be used for a jar of salsa where only the garlic is CNG, but not the tomatoes)
