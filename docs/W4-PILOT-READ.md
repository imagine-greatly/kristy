# Meat

## Beef

Old decision: 100% grass-fed or grass-finished, no added hormones, with a certifier’s seal.

New decision: 100% grass-fed or grass-finished beef, no added hormones, a certifier’s seal.

Old why: The whole-food standard is grass after weaning, no grain, and no feedlot.

New why: Grass after weaning, no grain and no feedlot. A grass finish gives you leaner beef with a different fat balance.

Old kristy_take: Whole-life words, backed by a seal, or it is a feedlot animal wearing a nicer sticker.

New kristy_take: Go gently with the heat: this leaner beef dries out at feedlot-beef heat. Look for whole-life words backed by a seal.

## Rotisserie chicken from the hot case

Old decision: Chicken and salt on the label: buy. Phosphate and starch: pass.

New decision: Rotisserie chicken with salt and spices, without phosphate or starch.

Old why: Every ingredient must be listed in descending order; a solution names its percent and contents.

New why: A plain bird is chicken, salt and spices, roasted in the store. Phosphate and starch go in with a needle.

Old kristy_take: A roasted chicken should list a chicken. When the list runs to phosphate and starch, the seasoning went in with a needle.

New kristy_take: Take a steaming bird from under the lamp and turn to the sticker. Every ingredient runs in descending order; a solution must name its percent and contents.

## Steak

Old decision: Chuck or round, whole, with no needles or blades pushed through.

New decision: Whole chuck or round, without needles or blades pushed through.

Old why: A cut pushed through needles or blades must say ‘mechanically tenderized’ in its name.

New why: Chuck is shoulder and round is rear leg: both are working muscles, sold whole.

Old kristy_take: The cut name says where on the animal it came from. ‘Mechanically tenderized’ in the name says what was done to it.

New kristy_take: The cut name tells you where on the animal the meat came from. Raw beef pushed through needles or blades must also say ‘mechanically tenderized’ in its name.

## Ground beef

Old decision: Organ blend with each organ’s percentage first, then ground beef with nothing added.

New decision: Organ blend with each organ’s percentage first, then nothing-added ground beef.

Old why: Federal rules keep added water, binders, and organs out of ‘ground beef’, so a blend carries its own name.

New why: An organ blend folds liver and heart into a familiar grind. Printed shares tell you how much of each.

Old kristy_take: The name is the check: ‘ground beef’ allows no added fat, water or binders, and ‘hamburger’ may add fat. A blend that prints its numbers is an honest pack. One that will not is a mystery bag.

New kristy_take: Federal rules keep added water, binders and organs out of ‘ground beef’, so a blend needs its own name. Plain ‘ground beef’ allows no added fat either; ‘hamburger’ may add fat.

## Eggs

Old decision: Pasture-raised, organic, and corn- and soy-free, all three together.

New decision: Eggs with pasture-raised, organic, corn- and soy-free on one carton.

Old why: Hens eat what they are given; the carton words show that feed and that life.

New why: A hen eats what she is given. Organic, pasture and feed words tell you about both her food and her life.

Old kristy_take: Feed, organic and pasture, all on one carton. The space words alone are the ones everybody sells.

New kristy_take: Pasture alone leaves the feed question open. Find all three claims together, then check the pack date: a fresh egg fries with a tall yolk and tight white.

## Judging meat at the case

Old decision: The pack with the newest ‘packaged on’ date in the case.

New decision: The meat pack with the newest ‘packaged on’ date.

Old why: Color is oxygen. A sealed package that is not leaking, and no off smell, tell you more.

New why: Oxygen turns a cut steak cherry-red; a vacuum-packed roast stays purple. Color alone cannot tell you freshness.

Old kristy_take: Color is oxygen, not quality. Check the seal, check for leaks, smell it. Those three beat the sticker.

New kristy_take: Choose a sealed pack without leaks, then smell the meat at home. No off smell or slime: those checks tell you more than the store’s sticker.

## What to ask the butcher

Old decision: Ask for beef soup bones, and a whole chuck ground fresh.

New decision: Beef soup bones and a whole chuck, freshly ground.

Old why: A staffed counter answers what a package never will, such as when the meat was cut.

New why: A whole chuck ground at the counter lets you choose the cut. The butcher can tell you when it was cut.

Old kristy_take: Ask what came in today, how it was raised and what was added.

New kristy_take: Start with what came in today, then how it was raised and what was added. Bones, trim and portioning are worth asking for while you are at the counter.

## Chicken

Old decision: Air-chilled whole chicken first, then bone-in, skin-on thighs.

New decision: Air-chilled whole chicken first, then bone-in, skin-on thighs.

Old why: Dark meat is forgiving and hard to overcook. Boneless skinless costs the most for the same bird.

New why: Thighs carry more fat and flavor, so they stay juicy and are hard to overcook. The skin renders and bastes as they cook.

Old kristy_take: Bone-in thighs are the most forgiving cut in the case. A whole bird costs least per pound, and the frame makes stock.

New kristy_take: A whole bird gives you breast, dark meat and a frame for stock. For thighs, keep the bone and skin: they give you more room at the stove than boneless skinless.

## Pork cuts and ‘solution added’ pork

Old decision: Pork whose label carries no added-solution percentage, only pork.

New decision: Plain pork, with no added-solution percentage on its label.

Old why: Raw meat with added solution must name it, give the percentage and list what is in it.

New why: Pork is often pumped with water, salt and phosphates to hold moisture. A plain pack leaves that solution out.

Old kristy_take: Read the fine print: ‘up to 12% solution’ is water sold by the pound.

New kristy_take: An ‘up to 12% solution’ line means some of that pack is added water. Raw meat with added solution must give its name, percentage and contents beside the product name.

## Whole turkey

Old decision: Whole turkey without an injected basting solution.

New decision: Plain whole turkey, without an injected basting solution.

Old why: An added basting solution is a separate formulation. A plain bird is turkey with nothing added.

New why: A plain turkey has nothing added. A basted bird can carry broth, stock, fat or water in an added solution.

Old kristy_take: An added basting solution is a separate formulation. Fresh or frozen, the plain bird is the pick.

New kristy_take: Fresh or frozen, read beside the name for a basting solution and its ingredients. Water left from chilling is a separate disclosure, so read the two lines separately.

## Ground turkey

Old decision: Plain ground turkey with its ingredients and fat content stated.

New decision: Plain ground turkey, with ingredients and fat content on the label.

Old why: Grinding does not specify the fat content. A plain pack is turkey with no seasoning or solution added.

New why: Two packs of ground turkey can have different fat contents. Read the fat line to see what you are taking home.

Old kristy_take: Grinding does not specify the fat content. So read the fat line, and take the pack with nothing added.

New kristy_take: Look for turkey on the ingredient list, with no added seasoning or flavoring solution. A percent-lean claim must have its percent-fat statement alongside.

## Chicken breast

Old decision: Plain breast, no added solution; bone-in and skin-on before boneless skinless.

New decision: Plain bone-in, skin-on breast before boneless skinless; no added solution.

Old why: Added flavoring solution changes the formulation. A plain breast carries nothing but the chicken.

New why: Bone-in, skin-on breast cooks better than boneless skinless, which dries fast in the pan.

Old kristy_take: Added flavoring solution changes the formulation. The product name has to admit it, so the plain pack is easy to find.

New kristy_take: A plain breast gives you only chicken. Added flavoring solution must appear in the product name with its amount, even on a pack labeled ‘natural’.

## Duck and quail eggs

Old decision: Cook duck and quail eggs until white and yolk are solid.

New decision: Clean, cold duck and quail eggs; cook white and yolk solid.

Old why: Salmonella incidents involving duck eggs arise from time to time. Thorough cooking is the safeguard for any egg.

New why: Duck eggs need longer to cook than hens’ eggs. Salmonella incidents are the reason to cook them thoroughly.

Old kristy_take: A duck or quail egg is still an egg. Buy it clean and cold, and cook it all the way through.

New kristy_take: Open the carton for clean, uncracked shells and keep the eggs cold at home. Duck or quail, cook until both yolk and white are firm.

## Lamb and goat

Old decision: 100% grass-fed or grass-finished lamb or goat.

New decision: Lamb or goat labeled 100% grass-fed or grass-finished.

Old why: Lamb quality grading is voluntary and takes age into account; goat meat has no quality grades.

New why: For goat, look at the flesh: firm and fine-grained with white fat. There are no goat quality grades to guide you.

Old kristy_take: Lamb quality grading is voluntary, and the grade takes age into account. Goat meat has no quality grades at all.

New kristy_take: Lamb grading is voluntary and takes age into account. Check for firm, fine-textured meat with white marbling, and read the cut name, such as shoulder or leg.

## Organ meats

Old decision: Organ meats bought for a cook within one to two days.

New decision: Organ meats for cooking within one to two days.

Old why: Variety meats keep a shorter time than steaks and roasts. Each organ also suits its own cooking method.

New why: Heart and tongue suit cooking in liquid. Liver can be pan-fried; tripe needs a braise to turn tender.

Old kristy_take: Variety meats keep a shorter time than steaks and roasts. Each organ also suits its own cooking method.

New kristy_take: Choose the organ with a meal in mind: variety meats keep less time than steaks and roasts. Cook within one to two days, and take all organ meats to 160°F.

## Bison

Old decision: 100% grass-fed or grass-finished bison, with an inspection mark.

New decision: Bison with 100% grass-fed or grass-finished labeling and an inspection mark.

Old why: Bison inspection can be federal or state. Once bought, bison keeps and cooks on the same rules as beef.

New why: Bison is a bovine meat with no marbling, so expect a deeper red than beef. High heat can turn it tough.

Old kristy_take: Bison is a bovine with no marbling. Find the inspection mark, then treat it like beef.

New kristy_take: Find a federal or state inspection mark, then follow beef’s keeping and cooking rules. Ground bison should be cooked or frozen within 2 days.

## Venison and game

Old decision: Venison from a fully refrigerated processor, trimmed of its fat.

New decision: Fat-trimmed venison from a fully refrigerated processor.

Old why: Game is often processed by small shops. Cold handling decides its safety.

New why: Game often comes through small shops. Cold handling at the processor decides its safety.

Old kristy_take: Game is only as good as its cold handling. Trim the fat, and cook it to temperature.

New kristy_take: Plan a meal within two or three days of bringing unfrozen venison home. Trim the fat and cook whole cuts to 145°F, ground game to 160°F.

# Bread

## Sandwich bread

Old decision: Whole wheat sandwich bread keeps the grain’s bran and germ.

New decision: Whole wheat sandwich bread, with the grain’s bran and germ intact.

Old why: Whole wheat flour retains the grain’s natural proportions. That is the whole-food standard for sandwich bread.

New why: Whole wheat flour keeps the bran and germ along with the rest of the grain, in their natural proportions.

Old kristy_take: Whole wheat flour retains the grain’s natural proportions. That is the whole-food standard for sandwich bread.

New kristy_take: A loaf called “wheat” may still use refined flour. Turn it over and find whole wheat flour in the ingredients before choosing it.

## Sourdough bread

Old decision: Sourdough fermented with a starter of wild yeast and bacteria.

New decision: Sourdough raised with a starter of wild yeast and bacteria.

Old why: The whole-food standard favors starter fermentation. A sour flavor alone does not establish that process.

New why: A flour-and-water starter lets wild yeast and bacteria raise the dough and develop its sour flavor.

Old kristy_take: The whole-food standard favors starter fermentation. A sour flavor alone does not establish that process.

New kristy_take: That sourness can also come from added acids. If the starter is missing from the list, the baker can tell you how the loaf rose.

## Bagels

Old decision: Bagels with their ingredients named, boiled before the oven.

New decision: Bagels boiled before the oven, with their ingredients named.

Old why: Boiling describes how the bagel is made. The ingredient list, or the baker, names what went into the dough and the bath.

New why: The boil sets a bagel’s outside and gives it that chew. A longer boil can make the crust chewier and the middle denser.

Old kristy_take: Boiling describes how the bagel is made. The archived articles support texture facts, not health effects.

New kristy_take: Ask what went into the dough and the boiling bath, or read the ingredients on the bag. Boiling explains the texture; the archived baking articles establish no health advantage.

## Corn vs. flour tortillas

Old decision: Corn tortillas made from lime-treated corn, water, and salt.

New decision: Corn tortillas made with lime-treated corn, water and salt.

Old why: Masa comes from corn processed with limewater. A simple corn recipe is the whole-food standard here.

New why: Masa is corn cooked and soaked in limewater, then ground into dough. Water and salt complete the tortilla recipe.

Old kristy_take: Masa comes from corn processed with limewater. A simple corn recipe is the whole-food standard here.

New kristy_take: Masa harina is the dried, ground dough; plain corn flour is a different ingredient. Check the bag for wheat too, since some corn tortillas contain both.

## Sprouted grain bread

Old decision: Sprouted grain bread with sprouted whole grains first, no added flour.

New decision: Sprouted whole grains first in your bread, without added flour.

Old why: Sprouted bread uses whole grains sprouted before milling. Some loaves add flour, and the ingredient list shows it.

New why: The grains sprout before milling, and the loaf tends to feel denser and heavier than bread made from flour.

Old kristy_take: The ingredient list settles it. Sprouted whole grains lead, and no extra flour follows.

New kristy_take: Look in the cold or freezer case for sprouted whole grains first on the list, with no extra flour following. Sprouted wheat still contains gluten.

## Gluten-free bread

Old decision: Gluten-free bread carrying the label claim, with its flours and binder read.

New decision: Gluten-free labeled bread, with its flours and binder checked.

Old why: Four label wordings share one federal gluten limit. The claim covers gluten only, so the flours and binder still matter.

New why: Blended flours and starches replace wheat. A gum binder gives back some of the stretch gluten would provide.

Old kristy_take: The claim settles the gluten. The ingredient list shows what holds the loaf together.

New kristy_take: Four label wordings share one federal gluten limit, and that claim covers gluten alone. Read which flours and binder hold this particular loaf together.

## The bread aisle

Old decision: Pick the bread type first, then read the first flour for “whole”.

New decision: Your chosen bread type, with “whole” in the first flour.

Old why: The first ingredient and the Whole Grain Stamp tell a whole grain loaf from a refined one.

New why: The first flour and the Whole Grain Stamp help you find a whole grain loaf among the refined ones.

Old kristy_take: The name on the front can mislead. The first ingredient is the read.

New kristy_take: “Wheat flour”, “multigrain” and “stoneground” on the front can leave the grain question open. “100% whole wheat” or “100% whole grain” gives you a clearer answer.

# Produce

## Produce

Old decision: Organic for produce eaten whole; conventional for produce you peel.

New decision: Organic produce eaten whole; conventional fruit and vegetables you peel.

Old why: Residue sits on the surface, so whatever gets thrown away takes it along.

New why: Surface residue leaves with a discarded peel. On berries and apples, you eat the skin along with the flesh.

Old kristy_take: Spend it on the ones eaten whole. Everything that gets peeled, buy plain.

New kristy_take: An avocado or kiwi can feel delicate and still have a peel you throw away. Choose conventional there; keep organic for the produce you eat whole.

## Frozen vs. fresh produce

Old decision: Frozen vegetables are picked ripe and frozen within hours.

New decision: Frozen vegetables, picked ripe and frozen within hours.

Old why: Picked ripe and frozen fast. ‘Fresh’ can lose more over days in transit.

New why: Freezing holds vegetables at the ripeness they were picked. ‘Fresh’ produce can lose nutrients over days in transit.

Old kristy_take: Half a freezer of frozen vegetables and berries is a fine way to live. Zero waste, half the price, always in season.

New kristy_take: Leave half a freezer for vegetables and berries to use in soups, smoothies and sheet-pan roasts. Plain frozen produce needs no additives and is there in every season, with no spoilage waste.

## Freezing what is about to turn

Old decision: Enzymes keep working in the freezer until heat switches them off.

New decision: Briefly scalded vegetables for the freezer; fruit goes in raw.

Old why: Enzymes keep working below freezing and turn color and flavor. A brief scald ends them.

New why: A brief scald stops the enzymes that would leave frozen beans gray and tasting of hay months later.

Old kristy_take: Sixty seconds in boiling water, then ice, then a tray. That is the whole method.

New kristy_take: Sixty seconds in boiling water, then straight into ice water. Dry the vegetables and freeze them on a tray before bagging, so the pieces stay separate.

## Buying produce in season

Old decision: Produce in season is picked closer to ripe and travels less.

New decision: In-season produce, picked closer to ripe with less distance to travel.

Old why: Abundance is the season showing. Out of season, frozen beats a pale expensive fresh.

New why: A January orange tastes better than a July one. Produce in season usually travels less and reaches you closer to ripe.

Old kristy_take: Buy the vegetables that are cheap and piled high this week. That’s what’s in season, and it’s where your money goes furthest.

New kristy_take: Let the piles in the produce section guide the week: spring greens, summer tomatoes, autumn squash, winter citrus. Off-season, reach for frozen over pale fresh produce.

## Washing produce

Old decision: Plain cold water removes as much residue as a produce wash does.

New decision: Plain cold water for produce, as effective as a produce wash.

Old why: Plain water is the recommendation. Rinse right before eating, not before storing.

New why: Cold water and a little rubbing lift dirt, microbes and some residue. Produce washes have not proved better.

Old kristy_take: Cold water and your hands. Skip the pricey ‘veggie wash’. It’s a solution to a problem you don’t have.

New kristy_take: Brush firm produce; rinse and spin leafy greens. Leave berries and mushrooms dry until you are ready to eat them, and skip the ‘veggie wash’.

## Getting residue off conventional produce

Old decision: Baking soda in the water lifts residue a plain rinse leaves.

New decision: Baking soda water for residue that a plain rinse leaves.

Old why: A rinse moves dirt. Bicarbonate breaks residue down, and it needs minutes to do it.

New why: Bicarbonate needs minutes to break down some residue on fruit skin. A quick rinse moves dirt.

Old kristy_take: A rinse is for dirt. If residue is the reason, it is a soak and it takes twelve minutes.

New kristy_take: Give the soak twelve to fifteen minutes, then rinse the soda off with plain water. Residue already inside the fruit stays there.

## Pre-cut and pre-washed produce

Old decision: Cutting produce starts the clock on vitamin loss and browning.

New decision: Whole produce before pre-cut: cutting starts vitamin loss and browning.

Old why: Cutting opens every surface at once. It costs more and it keeps for days, not weeks.

New why: Whole produce can wait for the day you need it. Once cut, it keeps for days rather than weeks.

Old kristy_take: Buy it whole and cut it yourself. Two minutes, and it keeps a week longer.

New kristy_take: Two minutes of cutting at home buys a week longer in the fridge. For pre-cut melon or pineapple, choose it on the day you will eat it.

## How to pick it, item by item

Old decision: The heavier piece of two the same size holds more juice.

New decision: The heavier of two same-size pieces of fruit for more juice.

Old why: Weight means water content, not pith. One habit that beats every color rule going.

New why: Compare two pieces in your hands. More weight means more water rather than pith, a better guide than color.

Old kristy_take: Pick up two and take the heavier one. That one habit beats every color rule going.

New kristy_take: Use your palm to feel a peach’s shoulder for a little give; fingertips leave bruises. Choose staggered firmness so the fruit ripens across the week.

## Berries

Old decision: Organic berries, and the carton’s underside decides, not the top.

New decision: Organic berries in a cold carton with a dry, clean underside.

Old why: Berries mold from the bottom up under their own weight, long before the top layer shows it.

New why: The bottom berries carry the weight and mold first, while the top can still look fresh.

Old kristy_take: Flip the carton. The top layer is the display and the bottom layer is the truth.

New kristy_take: Berries will not ripen further at home, so buy them ready. Turn the carton over: a dry bottom, no fuzzy fruit. Keep them cold and wash just before eating.

## Making produce last the week

Old decision: Washing produce before storing it traps moisture and speeds rot.

New decision: Unwashed produce for storing; wash it when you use it.

Old why: Moisture is what rots a drawer. Keep the ripening-gas fruit away from greens and berries.

New why: Water left on a leaf or berry is where mold starts. Keeping greens dry gives them longer in the drawer.

Old kristy_take: Wash it when you eat it, not when you unpack it. Half of what rots in a fridge was rinsed on the way in.

New kristy_take: Half of what rots in a fridge was rinsed on the way in. Store greens dry with a towel, away from ripening-gas fruit; stand herbs and asparagus in a little water.

## Reviving wilted greens

Old decision: Limp greens have lost water, not gone bad, and they come back.

New decision: Limp greens without decay, ready for a cold-water soak.

Old why: The cells have gone slack. Given cold water they refill and the leaf goes rigid again.

New why: Cold water refills a limp leaf’s slack cells. As the water returns, the leaf stands firm again.

Old kristy_take: Cold water and half an hour. Most of the greens in the bin did not need to be there.

New kristy_take: Give wilted greens half an hour in cold water, then dry them before putting them away. Yellowing, slime or a smell means decay; a soak cannot bring those leaves back.

## Sprouts

Old decision: Sprouts grow in the warm damp that bacteria also like.

New decision: Cold-case sprouts; warm, damp growing conditions also suit bacteria.

Old why: A living food sold warm is the only real problem. Cold case or your own jar.

New why: Crisp stems and white roots are what to look for in a cold clamshell. Sprouts go slick and sour before they look wrong.

Old kristy_take: Grow them yourself and the sourcing question answers itself.

New kristy_take: A jar and a mesh lid let you grow your own crop and know where it came from. Bought sprouts belong in the cold case; warm displays leave them in growing conditions.

## Root vegetables: carrots, potatoes, beets

Old decision: Organic potatoes, conventional carrots. Firm roots, nothing soft or sprouting.

New decision: Firm organic potatoes, conventional carrots, with nothing soft or sprouting.

Old why: Firm is the freshness signal every root shares. Each guide says to pass on the soft, shriveled, or sprouting ones.

New why: Potatoes, carrots, beets and sweet potatoes should feel firm. Soft, shriveled or sprouting roots stay behind.

Old kristy_take: Squeeze before anything else. Firm is the whole test at this bin, and a root that gives has already lost the argument.

New kristy_take: Wilted beet leaves need not put you off a firm root. Cut the tops off carrots before storing them; left attached, they wilt the carrot.

## Broccoli, cauliflower, and cabbage

Old decision: Conventional is fine on cabbage and cauliflower. Tight heads, no soft spots.

New decision: Conventional cabbage and cauliflower with tight heads and no soft spots.

Old why: Enlarged, opened, or yellowish-green florets mean broccoli past fresh, and a soft or slippery head is already spoiling.

New why: Tight, dark green broccoli florets are fresh. Enlarged, open or yellowish-green ones are past fresh.

Old kristy_take: Look at the florets first. Dark green and closed tight, and the rest of the head follows.

New kristy_take: A soft or slippery head is spoiling. Use broccoli early in the week; it keeps about three days, while wrapped cabbage can wait several weeks in the fridge.

## Onions and garlic

Old decision: Conventional onions and garlic: firm, dry, papery skins, with no sprouts or mold.

New decision: Conventional onions and garlic: firm, dry, papery skins, no sprouts or mold.

Old why: A well-cured bulb has a dry neck and dry outer skins. A soft, scented, or sprouting one is already on its way out.

New why: A well-cured bulb feels dry at the neck and papery outside. A soft, scented or sprouting bulb is already on its way out.

Old kristy_take: Squeeze the bulb and smell it. Firm and quiet is the one to take home.

New kristy_take: A few loose onion skins are normal. Squeeze for firmness and little scent, then store whole onions where air can move, away from damp potatoes.

## Leafy greens: lettuce, spinach, kale

Old decision: Organic greens, crisp and evenly colored, with no wilt or yellowing.

New decision: Crisp organic greens, evenly colored, without wilt or yellowing.

Old why: Spinach is highly perishable and kale wilts in the warm, so the crispness of the leaf is the freshness date.

New why: Spinach is highly perishable, and kale wilts in warmth. A leaf that still stands crisp tells you it has freshness left.

Old kristy_take: Pick the bunch that still stands up, and keep it cold from the cart to the crisper.

New kristy_take: For milder, more tender kale, choose the smaller leaves on moist stems. Keep the bunch cold from the cart to the crisper, and wash it when you use it.

## Bell peppers

Old decision: Organic peppers, firm and evenly colored, with no cracks or soft spots.

New decision: Firm organic peppers, evenly colored, without cracks or soft spots.

Old why: Firmness is a quality index on the postharvest sheet. A shriveled pepper has lost water in storage.

New why: A pepper should feel firm when you hold it. Wrinkled skin means it has lost water in storage.

Old kristy_take: A firm pepper is a fresh pepper; a shriveled one has already spent its shelf life.

New kristy_take: Red, yellow and orange peppers tolerate cold better than green ones. In a fridge at 41°F, plan on about two weeks; leave any cracked or bruised pepper at the bin.

## Apples and pears

Old decision: Organic apples and pears, unbruised. One spoiled fruit rots its neighbors faster.

New decision: Unbruised organic apples and pears, with no spoiled fruit alongside.

Old why: Damaged fruit spoils in storage, and spoiled fruit makes the sound apples and pears beside it ripen and rot faster.

New why: Apples should feel crisp. A firm green Bartlett pear can ripen at home, turning sweet, juicy and buttery.

Old kristy_take: One bruised apple in the bag costs the whole bag, so sort at the bin, not at home.

New kristy_take: Turn each fruit before bagging it. Damaged fruit spoils in storage and speeds ripening and rot in its sound neighbors, so leave bruised apples and pears behind.

## Citrus: oranges, lemons, limes

Old decision: Conventional citrus, organic for zest. Firm, smooth, no drying or shriveling.

New decision: Firm, smooth conventional citrus; organic for zest, without drying or shriveling.

Old why: Firm fruit with a sound rind is the quality index on the UC Davis citrus sheets. Decay and shriveling are the defects.

New why: Yellow limes carry more juice. A partly green orange can be fully mature, so feel for a firm, smooth rind.

Old kristy_take: Firm and smooth beats soft and shriveled on every citrus, and a green tinge on an orange is not a fault.

New kristy_take: UC Davis’s checks are firmness and a sound rind: leave decay and shriveling behind. Use yellowed lemons and limes first; green-picked ones keep longest.

## Stone fruit: peaches, nectarines, plums, cherries

Old decision: Organic peaches, plums, nectarines and cherries, unbruised, with color fully turned.

New decision: Unbruised organic peaches, plums, nectarines and cherries, with fully turned color.

Old why: Michigan Fresh gives the same instruction for peaches, plums, and cherries: buy fruit that is not bruised or damaged.
