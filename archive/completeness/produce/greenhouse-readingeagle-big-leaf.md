Big Leaf Hydroponic Lettuce & Greens is a small operation with lofty ambitions.

The goal, according to co-owner Wilson Zimmerman, is to serve the community by offering a top-quality product that is locally grown in nutrient-rich water without pesticides, harvested weekly and delivered to sales outlets the very next day. It’s called living lettuce because the roots are left intact, which makes it last longer.

“We think it benefits communities to know exactly where their food comes from and how it’s grown,” Zimmerman said. “Our vision is to be able to expand so we can provide pesticide free, locally grown salad for more people.”

After about a year in business, Big Leaf Hydroponics, 953 Dunkels Church Road, Greenwich Township, is harvesting between 800 and 1,000 units of lettuce a week, with Wednesday designated as pick-and-pack day. The firm practices integrated pest management and is certified GAP, good agricultural practices, a U.S. Department of Agriculture food safety program.

The lettuce, most of which is a summer crisp variety, is grown year-round and sold in several local stores, including Redner’s Markets.

“We grow some romaine and some bibb lettuce, but mostly the summer crisp because we felt like it has the best balance between crispiness and flavor,” Zimmerman said.

The lettuce, with its bright green leaves and clean, fresh appearance, is packaged in plastic containers and has a relatively long shelf life, lasting in the refrigerator for between two and three weeks, as long as the attached root remains damp.

Zimmerman learned the art of hydroponic agriculture from a farmer friend in Georgia and decided to try to make a go of it at his home. Hydroponic growing is up to 90% more water efficient than growing in soil.

The operation is a family effort, led by Zimmerman and his cousin/business partner, Steve Martin of Fleetwood. Zimmerman’s wife, Julia, and Martin’s wife, Elizabeth, join in on Wednesdays to pick and pack the lettuce.

And, although their combined six children are not allowed in the greenhouse for sanitary reasons, they enjoy watching through a window from an attached playroom.

Martin and Zimmerman last year helped an Amish crew build a large greenhouse on Zimmerman’s property.

Unlike some large hydroponic operations that grow plants indoors under artificial lighting, the greenhouse enables natural sunlight for the plants. The climate inside is rigorously controlled, with temperatures kept as close to between 65 and 70 degrees as possible.

“We use an auto-vent system that helps control the temperature,” Zimmerman said. “There’s an alarm system that sends an alarm to my smartphone if it gets too hot or too cold in here.”

Water running over cool cells helps reduce the heat in the summer, and an automatic shading system serves to block some of the sunlight to reduce heat, and also holds in heat during the winter.

The lettuce is grown in rainwater, which is collected and diverted into three 3,000-gallon tanks near the greenhouse.

The water is then taken through a filtration system, after which nutrients are added to feed the plants.

“We don’t just grow in water, we grow in nutrient-filled water,” Zimmerman said. “You have to get the balance of nutrients correct in order for your lettuce to be healthy. If you don’t figure that out, you won’t be successful.”

He tests the nutrients and the pH level of the water every day.

“That’s something that needs to be done every day,” he said. “It’s one of the most important parts of growing the lettuce.”

The greenhouse is heated with propane, but Zimmerman and Martin are seeking alternatives to reduce costs.

“If we can figure out how to cut down on heating costs, we’ll be able to increase our profitability,” Zimmerman said.

It takes eight weeks for lettuce to mature in the winter, and six weeks in the summer, Zimmerman explained. At any given time, about six weeks worth of lettuce is growing.

While keeping the nutrients in balance is Zimmerman’s No. 1 challenge, marketing the product is his second biggest concern.

Only about 60% of the lettuce grown in the greenhouse gets to a retail outlet. The rest is donated to soup kitchens or given away. For example, the cage-free hens at The Nesting Box Farm Store & Creamery, in Albany Township, eat the lettuce as a supplement to their feed.

“We definitely have room for expansion,” Zimmerman said. “We’d love to find some additional outlets to sell our lettuce.”

While hoping to grow the business, providing a service to the community will remain a priority.

“This definitely is not a get-rich-quick operation,” Zimmerman said. “It’s more about family working together and benefiting the community.”
