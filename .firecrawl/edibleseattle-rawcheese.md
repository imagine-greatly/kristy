# In the Raw

> Cascadia Creamery’s cave-aging process follows Old World traditions to create cheese with Cascadia terroir.

STORY BY NICOLE CAPOZZIELLO

PHOTOS BY CHARITY BURGGRAAF

![](https://www.edibleseattle.traceyryder.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/raw-cheese-wine.jpg)**“And this,” says John Shuman,** illuminating a small set of descending steps, “is the underground layer.”

My borrowed, too-large wading boots slap the stone floor as I follow John down into a cold room.

We wind around 4-foot-high stacks of cheese, each pallet of 6-pound wheels labeled with the name of one of Cascadia Creamery’s creations: Sleeping Beauty, Cloud Cap, Glacier Blue. We arrive at a small room in the back where, near the floor, is a 2-by-2-foot window. As I look crouch to look through the small window, I see another world: a lava-tube cave that predates us all, stumbled upon by John a decade ago, in a region with a cheesemaking history dating back nearly 150 years.

Beyond the walls of the creamery is the small town of Trout Lake, Washington. Idyllic even by Pacific Northwest standards, fields of organic herbs flourish and cattle graze, all in the shadow of Mt. Adams. It’s here that John and Marci Shuman have been making their organic, raw-milk cheese since 2010, selling their products throughout the area, from Portland’s New Seasons Markets to Seattle-area PCCs and Whole Foods.

John and Marci lived in Portland for a decade before making Trout Lake their home in 2008. “We adjusted to the energy of this place,” says John. “We didn’t expect it to adjust to us.” And in Trout Lake, they found so much to celebrate: a rich history of dairy and agriculture, an appreciation for hand-crafted, value-added products, and a lot of local pride.

Seeing that the two main industries in Trout Lake were organic herbs and organic dairy, John set out to learn about both, first working in business development for one of the herb growers. After that, John began an internship at a local dairy, Pearsons, a family of 5th– generation dairy farmers.

The Pearsons’ operation has survived for so long because of good animal husbandry practices and wise decision-making. Being in such a remote area — John tells me they were the last population center in the country to get grid-tied power, and not until the 1940s — has shaped the way the people of Trout Lake interact (there’s still bartering) and make decisions, prioritizing long-term solutions over quick fixes. For instance, because medication could be scarce, the need for healthy animals outweighed anything else, including higher milk volume. As a result, the Pearsons’ herd of heritage-breed cows is the product of decade after decade of this mentality — cows selected because they live longer (twice as long as the average cow) and produce milk with high butterfat and protein. The Pearsons also had the prescience to become an early adopter of organic practices — back in the ’80s — and are now part of Organic Valley co-op.

![](https://www.edibleseattle.traceyryder.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/raw-cheese-shumans.jpg) John and Marci Shuman of Cascadia Creamery

“John’s a mad scientist,” says Marci. “When something breaks, his response is, ‘I’ve got this’ or, ‘Let’s get creative!’ So working at the dairy was so natural for him.” The variety of tasks jibed with John, who’s done everything from ski instructing to house-flipping to product development at Nike. For two years, John soaked up all that he could on the Pearsons’ farm, from fixing equipment to the ins and outs of animal husbandry — all the while being paid in raw milk.

“I knew what I wanted: a raw, organic, high-quality cheese,” says John. “And since I couldn’t get it, I had to make it myself.” In this case, necessity was not only the mother of invention but also of obsession. Using Pearson milk, John began the pursuit of making cheese for himself and neighbors, dedicating part of his blacksmithing workshop to cheesemaking.

Cascadia Creamery grew naturally from John and Marci’s love of Trout Lake; John originally wanted to contribute something to the local bartering economy. “The whole birth of this creamery and of this concept is John’s way to integrate into the community and have roots in the history here,” says Marci.

As time went on, and demand for their cheese grew, the business grew organically, adding employees and expanding the facility. They went from making cheese in a pot to making cheese in a vat, learning to evolve their technology while still making the cheeses they wanted to make. “Each of the cheeses in our line-up are made because they please me,” says John. The development of the Glacier Blue was a particularly special accomplishment; John describes it as “an academic pursuit” to make a cheese that would be approachable for him and other blue cheese non-enthusiasts. The result is a blue cheese that is creamy and nutty, perfectly sweet and salty, and, in my opinion, transcendent.

Making good cheese, of course, starts with good milk. The average American cheesemaker uses milk that has been both homogenized and pasteurized. How much they know about the farms their milk comes from can be very little. Homogenization is a mechanical process that breaks down fat molecules in such a way that they stay suspended in the milk instead of rising to the top. It’s the norm in the dairy industry, leading to a product with a smoother consistency and a longer shelf-life. Pasteurization is the process of heating milk up to kill potentially harmful bacteria, while also lengthening the shelf-life.

Raw milk has not undergone either of these processes. As was done for thousands of years, Cascadia Creamery makes cheese with milk coming directly from the cows, having never even been refrigerated. By not heating the milk beyond the cow’s body temperature, the lactase digestive enzyme — along with other enzymes, probiotic bacteria, and vitamins — stays alive in the milk, making it easier for many people to digest.

However, making raw cheese is also more of a risk. “If you’re making raw cheese, you better know a lot about your milk,” says John, who has the benefit of his first-hand experience and total visibility to Pearsons’ practices. “Their milk is super clean and always well above industry standard. And when the milk is coming to a raw market like ours, they slow everything down and check everything twice.”

![](https://www.edibleseattle.traceyryder.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/raw-cheese-creamery.jpg) In Washington state, where it is legal to sell raw milk and raw cheese, there are rules to ensure the safety of the product. One of these regulations is that all raw cheese must be aged at least 60 days. This requirement affects the kind of cheeses Cascadia Creamery can make — no fresh cheeses like a fromage blanc — as well as their pace of production, because unlike other cheesemakers, they have to have space for cheese to age for 60 days.

Aging their cheeses in a lava tube cave creates an environment that, like every aspect of Cascadia Creamery’s business, embraces the natural gifts of Trout Lake. Not only does this practice require minimal power consumption (the ideal temperature and humidity of the underground layer is achieved from the attaching cave) but it is an important aspect of terroir as the airflow from the cave imparts its own unique characteristics, such as bacteria and fungi, on the cheeses’ natural rinds.

Unsurprisingly, using raw milk also means embracing the variables in the milk itself, leading back to the variables in a cow’s life. Throughout the year, what a cow eats changes, from alfalfa silage to wild clover or legumes — even affected by the dew present on the plants — leading to the presence of different bacteria. Milk quality also changes from season to season. As wintertime approaches, cows produce richer, fattier milk with more calories for their calves.

“Because of the seasonality and cultures in the milk, we need to work with a recipe that embraces those, that uses those cultures as an asset,” says John. On the cheesemaking end, they’re constantly adjusting to the varying fat or moisture contents. “There is not one batch of cheese in the cave that tastes the same. I love the variance — each one has this personality.”

For Marci’s role in sales and marketing, she does a lot of cheesemonger and consumer education. While Cascadia’s products are consistently good, cheesemongers help customers understand that the creamery’s cheeses are a product of the environment and thus will change with the seasons. The rinds may look different, or a batch might taste slightly more mushroomy or grassy than the last.

“There’s this new wave of environmentally and nutritionally responsible, locally supportive, conscious consumers,” says Marci, who, from the beginning, has appreciated the support of local markets that are excited to source and sell local, raw, organic cheese with heart and a story. “We’re lucky to be in the right place at the right time, with the right mindset. And you don’t have demand without great products — great cheese made from great milk.”
